Most Punjab districts are one landscape with suburbs. Khushab is three landscapes stapled into one file. On the north-west the Salt Range lifts into Soon Valley — orchards, salt lakes, Sakesar at 1,522 metres. In the middle the Jhelum runs past the old town that gave the district its name: khush aab, sweet water. On the south-west the Thal sand begins, gram fields and empty miles toward Bhakkar. The administrative capital is not Khushab city. It is Jauharabad, a planned town from 1953. The district file itself is young: carved out in 1982 after Shahpur District had already been split in 1960. Babur, writing in the sixteenth century, already listed Khushab with Bhera and Chiniot as a frontier mark between Hindustan and Kabul. The name is older than the deputy commissioner’s office.

PBS 2023: 1,501,089 people on 6,511 km². Density only about 231 per km² — a thin sheet next to Kasur or Khanewal. Households 248,304. Urban 418,745 (28 percent). Rural 1,082,344. Under-tens about 372,000. Literacy at ten and above: 62.52 percent (men 75.59, women 49.03). The gap between those two numbers is wider than the Soon Valley is long. Official site: khushab.punjab.gov.pk.

The three belts, four tehsils

Khushab tehsil — 2,115 km², 816,682 people, literacy 65.94 percent. The Jhelum-side majority. Khushab city about 140,000. Jauharabad about 113,000. Hadali, Mitha Tiwana, Girote. Tiwana towns and the river road.

Noorpur Thal — 2,500 km², 264,597 people, literacy 55.58 percent. Largest tehsil by empty land. Gram, sand, the Thal edge. Town of Noorpur about 20,000. This is where density falls to 106 people per km².

Quaidabad — 1,080 km², 274,959 people, literacy 55.11 percent. Named in the 1950s. Weakest literacy of the four. Salt-range foot and Thal shoulder. Town about 24,500.

Naushera (Wadi-e-Soon) — 816 km², 144,851 people, literacy 70.43 percent. Smallest tehsil, best school row. The valley tehsil: Naushera, Khabeki, Uchhali, Angah, Khura. Created as its own tehsil in 2013 because the valley is not the plain and not the sand.

Neighbours: Sargodha, Jhelum, Chakwal, Mianwali, Bhakkar, Jhang. A federal research installation west of Jauharabad sits on this map; students will see the name in newspapers. This page does not treat it as a tourist stop.

Soon Valley first

Start here if you only have one afternoon. Uchhali, Khabeki and Jahlar are the three lakes. Khabeki is salt water, a kilometre across. Sakesar is the roof of the Salt Range; the Amb temples stand on that massif. Kanhatti Garden near Khabeki is the forest name. Padhrar to Sakesar is a thirty-five-mile trough of orchards and villages that do not look like the Thal and do not look like Sargodha’s citrus grid. Awan and other hill families farm the terraces. The PAF presence on Sakesar is part of the modern skyline. The valley is why people from Lahore have heard of Khushab at all. It is not why most Khushabis eat.

Thal and the river

Most of the cultivated acreage is still wheat, gram, fodder and a little cane and rice. Noorpur Thal grows gram on sand. The Jhelum side takes canal and river water and grows the denser crop. Mitha Tiwana and the old Tiwana forts are the political geology of the plain: a clan that took land from the late Mughal vacuum, served Ranjit Singh, then the British, and still prints names on assembly lists. Fateh Khan Tiwana is the nineteenth-century landlord name. The clan story is not the whole district. It is the story the plain tells about itself.

People from these three belts

Khushwant Singh (1915–2014) was born in Hadali, eight kilometres west of Jauharabad, in a Khatri Sikh house. Train to Pakistan is the novel the rest of the world opened. He left at Partition, came back once in the winter of 1987, spoke the local dialect to a schoolyard, and asked that some of his ashes be scattered there. A plaque in the village still says the roots were watered with nostalgia. Hadali would be one more Thal qasba without that book. With it, the sand has a paragraph in Indian and Pakistani classrooms at the same time.

Ahmed Nadeem Qasmi (1916–2006) — poet, short-story writer, editor of Funoon — was born in Angah in Soon Valley. Urdu’s twentieth-century Punjab has few cleaner sentences than his. The valley produced the man; Lahore printed the magazines.

Wasif Ali Wasif (1929–1993) — teacher, essayist, the short spiritual paragraph that still circulates on phones — was from Khushab. Students meet him as a quotation. The town meets him as a local son who wrote in a register that does not need a party flag.

Sultan Khan (c. 1903–1966), the chess master who beat the best Europeans of the early 1930s with almost no book knowledge of Western theory, came from the Mitha Tiwana countryside. He played for British India, returned, and died obscure. The board is the other thing this district exported besides wheat and sentences.

Sohail Warraich is the television journalist name. Idris Azad and Abdulqadir Hassan are the later writers. On the political sheet: the Tiwana ministers (Khuda Buksh, Ehsan Ullah), Sumaira Malik, Naeem Khan Awan, Shakir Bashir Awan. Sir Sobha Singh, builder of much of New Delhi, and his brother Ujjal Singh were Hadali names that made their lives in India after 1947. Some lists add Feroz Khan Noon; the Noon house is more tightly tied to the old Shahpur / Sargodha file than to this one. Mention him as a neighbour of the story, not as a Khushab birth certificate.

Speech

Punjabi about 92 percent, in the Shahpuri belt that also covers Sargodha. Saraiki about 2.4 percent on the Thal fringe. Pashto about 2 percent. Urdu about 1.4 percent. This is not Multan’s Saraiki pull and not Lahore’s Urdu office. It is river Punjabi with a hill accent in Naushera and a sand accent in Noorpur.

Colleges and the hospital

Government colleges in Jauharabad, Khushab, Naushera, Quaidabad and Noorpur. No public university of its own. Sargodha and Khushab’s own degree colleges hold the first years; medicine and engineering still mean Sargodha or Lahore. DHQ Jauharabad is the public hospital. THQs cover the other tehsils. Soon Valley roads close in monsoon slide years. Thal roads close in dust.

Towns on the three maps

Jauharabad, Khushab city, Hadali, Mitha Tiwana, Girote, Quaidabad, Noorpur Thal, Naushera, Khabeki, Uchhali, Angah, Padhrar.

Khushab is a Salt Range valley, a Jhelum town and a Thal tehsil asked to share one deputy commissioner. The 2023 sheet is one and a half million people, a women’s literacy row still under half, and a list of writers out of all proportion to the size of Jauharabad. The lakes photograph well. The gram field does not. Both are the district.
