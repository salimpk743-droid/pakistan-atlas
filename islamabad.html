Islamabad

Federal capital · planned in the 1960s · 2,363,863 people in 2023 · Margalla Hills to the north, Rawalpindi to the south

Islamabad is Pakistan’s federal capital. It is not a province. It is its own territory, drawn on the Potohar bench under the Margalla Hills so the state could leave the heat and crowding of Karachi. Karachi remained the commercial giant. Rawalpindi kept the cantonment and the old bazaar. Islamabad was told to be the quiet planned city: secretariat, presidency, parliament, foreign missions, and a grid of sectors with trees between them.

People who like planned capitals put this city in the same conversation as Canberra or Brasília — a seat of government set apart from the biggest port. That is a planning fact, not a tourist slogan. The beauty here is specific: the grey-green Margallas after rain, the white tent of Faisal Mosque against the slope, the long avenues of the Blue Area, and the villages that were here before the grid. Smog, unfinished housing societies and a jammed Kashmir Highway are also the city. Both sides belong on the same page.

How the capital was drawn

In the early 1960s the state chose this site. Constantinos Apostolou Doxiadis, the Greek planner, produced the master plan: a grid stepping south from the hills, zones for administration, diplomacy, housing, industry and a national park. Construction began while Ayub Khan was in office. Ministries moved up from Rawalpindi in stages. The Capital Development Authority became the landlord and the building policeman.

Sectors were lettered. F and G series became the first residential names people still use. The Red Zone holds the presidency, parliament and the diplomatic enclave. The Blue Area is the commercial strip. The National Park and the Margalla Hills National Park were meant to stop the city eating the ridge. They have not fully stopped it. Trails 3 and 5 are the walking paths residents name without a brochure.

Saidpur was a village before it was a restaurant row. Shah Allah Ditta keeps caves and old trees at the western foot of the hills. Golra Sharif is the shrine and the railway museum. Bari Imam is the Urs crowd on the Banigala side. Those four names are older than any sector number.

2023 numbers

PBS 2023: Islamabad Capital Territory 2,363,863. The 2017 figure was 2,003,368. Annual growth in that interval is about 2.8 percent — migration as much as births. The metropolitan corporation row for the city proper is about 1,108,872. The rest lives in the rural and peri-urban belt: fields, old villages, and housing schemes that were not on Doxiadis’s first sheet.

Area of the territory is about 906 km². Density is high for a “garden capital” because the inhabited belt is a fraction of that map. Mother-tongue rows in the ICT census report put Punjabi first (about half), then Pashto (about 18 percent), Urdu (about 12 percent), Hindko, Saraiki and Kashmiri after that. The capital is a meeting city. It is not a single-tongue town.

Rural ICT is still Potohar: Rajput and other local names in the mauzas, wheat and small livestock, a Friday mosque older than the secretariat. Urban ICT is officers, students, drivers, cooks, diplomats and the labour that keeps those houses standing.

What the hills and the mosque actually look like

The Margallas are the north wall. After the monsoon they hold a green that Karachi never sees. In dry May they go pale. Leopards still use the park; that is a wildlife fact CDA and the park staff already print, not a legend. Daman-e-Koh and Pir Sohawa are the viewpoints. On a clear winter morning you see the grid. On a smog morning you see nothing past F-7.

Faisal Mosque, designed by Vedat Dalokay, is the building visitors photograph. King Faisal of Saudi Arabia’s name is on it. The prayer hall sits at the foot of the ridge like a white camp. It is a working mosque, not only a postcard. Nearby sectors hear the azan without a loudspeaker war because the site is open.

Monal and the other ridge restaurants are the evening traffic jam. Saidpur’s painted houses are a restored village, useful and a little staged. Rawal Lake is the reservoir south of the parkway; it is water supply first, picnic second. None of this makes Islamabad “the most beautiful capital on earth” as a contest score. It makes it a rare planned capital that still has a wild ridge inside the municipal eye-line. That is enough praise, and it is checkable.

Sectors, bazaars, the twin city

Residents speak in sector codes: F-6 Super Market, Jinnah Super in F-7, Aabpara, Melody, G-9 Karachi Company, I-8 Markaz, F-10. Each markaz is a small town of banks, tailors and tuition centres. The Sunday bazaar shifts by sector. Blue Area at dusk is offices emptying onto the service road.

Rawalpindi is the other half of daily life. People work in Islamabad and sleep in Saddar, or the reverse. The highway between them is one labour market. Murree Road, IJP and the Kashmir Highway take the punishment. When it rains, underpasses pond. When it does not rain for a month, the hills look like cardboard and the taps weaken in the new societies.

Bari Imam’s Urs fills the Banigala road. Golra’s Thursday crowd is quieter. Christian and Hindu families are a small census row and an old part of Rawalpindi-Islamabad life; their churches and mandirs are in the twin-city file, not only in textbook minorities paragraphs.

Campuses and hospitals

Quaid-i-Azam University sits against the hills. NUST, COMSATS, IIUI, NUML, PIEAS and the federal colleges pull students from every province. Hostels are short. The Faisal Mosque campus of IIUI is a city of its own on the west. School chains — public federal schools and the expensive private ones in F and E sectors — split the city’s children into two futures. That split is an education fact, not a lifestyle article.

PIMS is the public giant. Polyclinic takes the civil servants’ load. Private teaching hospitals in G and F sectors take whoever can pay. Trauma from the highway is ordinary work. Fog and dust keep the chest wards busy in winter.

Problems that are not scenery

Housing societies past the original grid ate farmland and nullahs. Water tankers are a business. Margalla trails get new concrete every few years; the park loses a little each time. Encroachments on streams flood Aabpara and I-9 when the cloud bursts. Katchi abadis on the edges have no sector letter and no regular CDA file.

Traffic is the daily tax. Diplomatic convoys close a road without notice. The metro bus helped one corridor and left the rest to wagons. Air in December can be as dirty as any bigger city. A capital that sells clean air on a brochure owes the reader that winter sentence.

Security walls after the 2000s changed how the Red Zone feels. Ordinary theft and snatching are what the thana files when there is no headline. Mixing every crime into “capital politics” helps no one.

Public names

Islamabad is a workplace city. Presidents, prime ministers and service chiefs live here while they hold office. That does not make them “Islamabad families” in the Peshawar-haveli sense. Doxiadis is the planner’s name. Dalokay is the mosque architect. CDA chairmen come and go; residents remember the ones who cut or saved a tree line.

Writers, anchors and civil servants settle in F and G sectors because the job is here. Students who later become known often list a hometown in Punjab or KP and an Islamabad campus. Quaid-i-Azam University and NUST alumni lists are long; they are not a single caste of “Islamabad personalities.”

Sports grounds — Pindi cricket stadium sits in Rawalpindi, Jinnah Sports Stadium in the capital — host national sides. The athlete is usually from somewhere else. Say that plainly. A fake local-star list would be worse for this page than a short honest one.

Saidpur and Shah Allah Ditta keep Potohar family names that predate 1960. Those households are the oldest continuous residents. The officer who arrived in 1985 is also a resident. Both counts are true.

How a working day feels

Morning on the Kashmir Highway is headlights and school vans. By ten the secretariat car parks are full. At lunch Blue Area fills. Late afternoon the trails take office walkers. Night in F-7 is restaurants; night in a new society past the toll is dark plots and a guard hut. Friday sermons differ by sector. Independence Day flags on Constitution Avenue are the state photograph. The rest of the year the same avenue is a closed road with gardeners.

Rain in July turns the hills into the reason people stay. A dry June is when they talk about leaving for the hills or for Murree. Winter nights are cold enough for a heater in G-11 and a wood stove in a village on the Simly side. That range — heater and stove — is the territory.
