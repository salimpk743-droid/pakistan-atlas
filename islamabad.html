Federal Capital of Pakistan

Islamabad

A planned capital beneath the Margalla Hills — where government, universities, forests, old villages, diplomatic life and modern Pakistan meet in one territory.

Islamabad is more than a collection of numbered sectors. The capital contains a modern planned city, an enormous rural belt, historic Potohari settlements, forests, archaeological sites, religious shrines, universities and one of Pakistan's most recognisable mountain landscapes.

Status Federal Capital

Pakistan's Capital

The planned city that never stopped being a landscape

Islamabad was selected as Pakistan's new capital site in the late 1950s and developed during the 1960s. The city's master plan was prepared by the Greek planner Constantinos A. Doxiadis.

The result was a city organised around sectors, broad avenues, green belts and separated land uses, facing the Margalla Hills to the north.

But beneath that modern plan remained an older Potohari landscape: villages, fields, shrines, wells, caves, family compounds and ancient routes.

02
History

From Potohar landscape to national capital

Before Islamabad

An older Potohari landscape

Long before the modern capital, the area contained villages, agricultural land, religious sites, caravan routes and archaeological remains. Saidpur, Shah Allah Ditta, Golra, Nurpur Shahan and other settlements preserve parts of that older landscape.

1958

The capital site is selected

A commission examined possible locations for a new federal capital. The site north-east of Rawalpindi was selected because of its climate, location, natural setting and strategic considerations.

1960

The Doxiadis master plan

Greek planner Constantinos A. Doxiadis prepared the master plan. The city was organised into sectors and specialised zones, with the Margalla Hills forming the dramatic northern backdrop.

1963

Islamabad becomes the capital

Islamabad formally emerged as Pakistan's federal capital, gradually taking over administrative functions from Karachi.

1960s–today

A city that continues to expand

The capital grew from a carefully planned administrative centre into a major metropolitan territory containing residential sectors, universities, businesses, villages and expanding peri-urban areas.

03
Urban design

A city built around the sector

The Doxiadis idea

Islamabad was planned as a modern capital rather than an organically grown old city.

Residential sectors were designed as relatively self-contained neighbourhoods, connected by major roads and separated by green spaces.

The Margalla Hills provided a natural northern boundary and a permanent visual identity.

Nature

The Margalla Hills — Islamabad's northern wall

A forest inside the capital

The Margalla Hills give Islamabad its distinctive skyline and ecological identity.

The hills become intensely green during and after the monsoon and much drier during the hottest part of the year.

The park supports a variety of wildlife, including wild boar, monkeys, birds and the occasional leopard.

People

A meeting place of languages

Islamabad is one of Pakistan's most diverse urban populations. Punjabi, Pashto and Urdu are among the most widely represented languages, alongside Hindko, Saraiki, Kashmiri and other languages brought by migrants from across the country.

The rural belt retains stronger local Potohari and Punjabi identities, while the planned city contains a national mixture of civil servants, diplomats, students, businesspeople, professionals and workers from almost every part of Pakistan.

A working capital

How a day in Islamabad feels

Morning traffic enters the city from Rawalpindi, Bhara Kahu, Tarlai, Koral and the southern suburbs. School vans fill the main roads while government offices begin their working day.

By midday, Blue Area and the major markaz are busy with offices, banks, restaurants and shoppers. Universities create another rhythm around QAU, NUST, COMSATS, IIUI and NUML.

In the late afternoon, residents move toward parks, walking tracks and Margalla trails. F-6 and F-7 become restaurant and café districts after sunset.

At the same time, rural Islamabad continues its own rhythm of fields, livestock, mosques, village markets and family compounds.

18
Environment

Protecting the capital's green identity

Islamabad's greatest natural advantage is its combination of forested hills, open green spaces, water reservoirs and relatively low-density planned neighbourhoods.

The Margalla Hills and their wildlife are especially important. Islamabad's identity would be fundamentally different without the forested ridge immediately north of the city.

Responsible development therefore requires protection of forests, streams, nullahs, agricultural land and historic villages alongside carefully planned urban growth.

گرمی موسم گرم اور بعض اوقات مرطوب ہوتا ہے، جبکہ مون سون بارشیں بعد میں سبزہ بڑھاتی ہیں۔
مون سون شدید بارشیں مارگلہ پہاڑیوں کو انتہائی سرسبز بنا دیتی ہیں اور بعض اوقات اچانک سیلاب بھی آ سکتا ہے۔
