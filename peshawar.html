Peshawar District

Provincial capital · 4,758,762 people in 2023 · city, cantonment and a ring of tehsils that are not the walled town

Peshawar District: Quick Answers

Peshawar District is one of the 38 districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, in Peshawar Division, with its headquarters at Peshawar. The 2023 census lists 7 administrative units in it: Badhber, Cham Kani, Hassan Khel, Mathra, Peshawar, Pishta Khara and Shah Alam. The 2023 census counted 4,758,762 people on 1,518 km², and 53.28% of residents aged 10 and over were literate.

Peshawar is the city people mean when they say Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and it is also a district that spills far past the old gates. The walled town is one piece. Hayatabad is another. Chamkani, Mathra, Shah Alam, Peshtakhara, Badbher and Hassan Khel are the tehsil names that sit on the same PBS sheet as the municipal corporation. If you only write about Qissa Khwani you have left out more than half the district.

Pashto is the language of almost the whole count — about 4.42 million speakers in the 2023 compilation. Hindko still has a real row, about 180,000. Urdu about 85,000. Punjabi about 26,000. The city has always taken in other tongues. It did not stop being a Pukhtun city because of that.

Peshawar: What the 2023 book actually says

District population 4,758,762 on 1,518 km². That is more than three thousand people on every square kilometre if you flatten the map, which you should not, because Hassan Khel is not University Town. Households run into the hundreds of thousands. Males 2,425,808, females 2,332,767, transgender 187 in that compilation. Rural 2,852,787. Urban 1,905,975. So the district is still more village and peri-urban belt than municipal core, even though the core is huge.

Municipal corporation figure for Peshawar city: about 1,837,741 in 2023, a little under the 2017 municipal figure of about 1,893,344. Cantonment about 63,978. University town council a few thousand. Those three urban labels do not add up to the district. The rest lives in the tehsils.

Literacy on the 10-and-above row is not a proud round number. About 1.81 million listed as literate, 1.59 million not. Under-tens are about 1.36 million. A city that prints itself as a university capital still has that child-and-illiteracy weight. Female schooling in the outer tehsils is the part that does not show up on a postcard of Islamia College.

Peshawar: A short history that is not a textbook paragraph

The name is old. Purushapura in the Buddhist and Kushan centuries, then the Muslim town on the same fan of the Bara and the Kabul rivers. Babur knew it. The Mughals used it. The Durranis treated it as a winter capital more than once. The Sikhs took it. The British made it the frontier station: cantonment, railway, Edwardes College, Islamia College in 1913, the university later. None of that is folklore invented for this website. It is why the clock tower and the college dome still sit in the same photograph.

Qissa Khwani — the storytellers’ street — is the bazaar that swallowed caravans from Kabul and from India. In April 1930 the British firing there became a Congress and Khudai Khidmatgar memory. People still point at the street when they talk about that day. They also buy cloth there on an ordinary Thursday. Both uses of the street are real.

1947 made Peshawar a Pakistani provincial city. The old Hindu and Sikh mohallas thinned. Afghan wars after 1979 poured refugees into camps and then into the city itself. Hayatabad was planned as a clean township and then filled, like every planned township in this country, with relatives and shops that the original map did not draw. Karkhano grew as the market that lived off the Khyber road. Bara Road became a sentence, not just a direction.

The 2000s and 2010s are the chapter this page will not skip. Peshawar was not a distant “agency.” It was the city where the bombs arrived.

Peshawar: Bazaars a visitor actually walks

Start inside the gates if the traffic lets you. Kohati Gate, Hashtnagri, Andar Shehr, the lanes off Qissa Khwani. Copper and leather and cloth still have their streets. Namak Mandi is food and noise. Meena Bazaar is the women’s market people name without thinking. Saddar is the cantonment-facing row of shops, different accent, different rents. University Road and Jamrud Road are the linear bazaars of the new city — photocopy shops, phone shops, cheap clothes, medical stores stacked three deep.

Hayatabad’s sectors have their own small markets. They look nothing like Andar Shehr. That is the point of a township. Karkhano, toward the west, is the market that lived off the Agency road: imported cloth, electronics, the stuff that used to come through Landi Kotal when the crossing was a living. When Torkham closes, Karkhano feels it before Saddar does.

Fruit and vegetable wholesale still wakes before dawn. The grain mandi still argues in Pashto at a volume that sounds like a fight and is usually a price. Boarding houses around the bus stands take students from Dir and Waziristan and Bajaur. Those students eat at the same namak-mandi stalls as clerks from the secretariat. That mix is the city. A cleaned-up “heritage walk” that ignores the bus stand has not described Peshawar.

Peshawar: Tehsils outside the postcard

Peshawar City tehsil — 176 km², 2,113,596 people in the 2023 tehsil row, density printed above 12,000 per km². This is the municipal and cantonment weight. Literacy on that tehsil’s 10-plus row is higher than the outer belt, still not universal.

Chamkani — 226 km², 624,354 people, dense. Eastern approach. Not a village anymore.

Shah Alam — 145 km², 532,848 people.

Mathra — 218 km², 495,059 people.

Peshtakhara — 135 km², 480,436 people.

Badbher (Badhber) — 357 km², 439,912 people.

Hassan Khel — 261 km², 72,557 people. The thin tehsil. Treat it as the reminder that “Peshawar district” is not only Hayatabad Phase 7.

Neighbourhood councils and village councils run into the hundreds on local-government lists. This page will not pretend to name them all.

Peshawar: Schools, colleges, hospitals

Islamia College is the red-brick photograph. University of Peshawar sits behind it. Khyber Medical College, UET, Islamia College University, the private campuses on Warsak Road and in Hayatabad — the city collects students from the whole province. That is a fact and a pressure. Hostels are short. Transport is worse than the prospectus admits.

Lady Reading Hospital is the old public giant. Hayatabad Medical Complex is the other. Khyber Teaching Hospital sits with the medical college. When a bomb went off in this city, those three names filled the television crawl. They still take the ordinary load: road accidents, births, gunshot wounds that have nothing to do with politics, and the winter chest infections that come with the smog.

Government schools in the inner city are crowded. Private English-medium schools in Hayatabad sell a different future at a different fee. The gap between those two systems is an issue, not a colour piece.

Peshawar: The years the city was a target

After 2001 Peshawar sat next to the war in Afghanistan and next to the agencies that later became districts. Armed groups that the Pakistani state listed as terrorist organisations treated the city as a stage. Markets, mosques, churches, hotels, buses and schools were hit. This page names the public events. It does not explain how a device is made.

Pearl Continental, in June 2009, was one of the early hotel attacks that told the rest of the country the provincial capital was not a safe rear area. All Saints Church, in September 2013, killed dozens of worshippers in Kohati. That Sunday is still a date in the Christian mohalla. Other mosques and imambargahs were hit in the same decade. Shopkeepers in Qissa Khwani learned the sound of a blast from the next street and the smell that follows. They opened the shutters the next morning because rent does not wait.

16 December 2014 is the date this city will not retire. Army Public School. One hundred and thirty-two students and staff among the dead, on the official count that the country memorised. The school is in the cantonment belt. The funeral prayers filled the city. The rest of Pakistan used the word “our children.” In Peshawar the children had names and streets and tuition centres. Security walls went up around every school that could afford bricks. Bags were checked. Parents waited at gates in a way they had not waited in 1998. Some families moved a child to a school farther from the main roads. Some left the city. Most stayed, because this is home.

Attacks did not begin in 2014 and did not end that afternoon. Checkposts multiplied. Markets lost evening hours. Wedding halls hired searches. Journalists and policemen were killed in ones and twos that never got a national minute of silence. The Afghan-refugee neighbourhoods were blamed in cheap talk; the bombers did not need a refugee camp to hate a school. Say that plainly.

Later years were quieter on some streets and not on others. The city did not “defeat terrorism” like a banner. It learned to live with walls, and then to hate the walls, and then to keep them anyway. A student writing a clean ending here would be lying.

Peshawar: Ordinary problems that are not explosions

Drainage. After rain the inner city ponds. Sewage meets rainwater in places that still use drains drawn for a smaller town. Smog in winter sits on the basin. Traffic on GT Road, Ring Road and Jamrud Road is a daily tax on hours. Unplanned housing ate the orchards on the edges. Water from tube wells is a fight between sectors. Katchi abadis on the river side flood when the Kabul River rises.

Refugees and former refugees are part of the labour market and the school rolls. When a repatriation drive starts, certain bazaars thin. When it pauses, they fill. That is not a morality play. It is how this city has worked since the 1980s.

Local government rearranges town names every few years. The man selling pomegranates on the same crate does not move. Write policy as policy. Write the crate as the crate.

Crime that is not “terrorism” — snatching, land grabbing, the drug trade in named colonies — is what the thana actually files on a Tuesday. Mixing every crime into the 2014 chapter helps no one.

Peshawar: How the city feels on a working day

Summer is brutal. Winter mornings are fog and smoke. Friday prayers empty the shops for an hour and then return them louder. In Ramzan the bazaars wake at night. In Muharram some routes close. In Eid the bus stands burst with people going back to villages in Charsadda and to towns in Dir. None of that needs a poet. It needs a bus that leaves on time.

Pashto newspaper offices, FM radios, the old cinema halls that became marriage halls — the culture is not only the museum. It is also the cheap mobile film and the wedding video shot in Hayatabad Park. Hindko families in the inner city still keep their speech at home. That layer is thinner than it was in 1972. It is not gone.

Peshawar: Families and public names

Qissa Khwani and the inner mohallas sent more film names to Bombay than any other street in this province. Yusuf Khan — Dilip Kumar — is the name the city repeats first. Prithviraj Kapoor and Raj Kapoor are tied to Kapoor Haveli in the same old town. Shah Rukh Khan’s father, Meer Taj Mohammed Khan, is listed in city notes as a Qissa Khwani man before Bombay. Amjad Khan’s family is also claimed from this belt. Those are published origin claims. The actors themselves grew up and worked in India.

On this side of the border the screen names from Peshawar include Qavi Khan, Firdous Jamal, Rasheed Naz, Ajab Gul and Ismail Shahid. They are the Pakistani film and television row, not the Bombay row.

Squash put another kind of name on the map: Jansher Khan is the Peshawar champion the world already knows. Rahman Baba’s shrine in the city is why the poet is treated as a Peshawar name even though the verse is older than the municipal boundary. Bacha Khan’s movement ran through this city; his home district is Charsadda. Keep that line straight.

Old Hindu and Sikh trading houses of the inner city thinned after 1947. Their shops and havelis are part of the same bazaar history as the Kapoor house. A student page that only lists film stars has missed the merchants who paid for the street.

Peshawar District: FAQ

What is the population of Peshawar District?

The 2023 census counted 4,758,762 people in Peshawar District, up from 4,331,959 in 2017 (annual growth 1.58%). 40.05% of the population lived in urban areas.

What is the area of Peshawar District?

Peshawar District covers 1,518 km² according to the 2023 census, a density of 3,134.89 people per km².

What are the main towns and cities in Peshawar District?

The 2023 census lists 3 urban localities in Peshawar District: Peshawar (1,837,741, municipal corporation), Peshawar Cantonment (63,978, cantonment) and Peshawar University (4,256, town committee).

What is the literacy rate of Peshawar District?

53.28% of people aged 10 and over were literate in the 2023 census (male 64.91%, female 41.09%).

Which languages are spoken in Peshawar District?

The most common mother tongue in the 2023 census was Pashto (93.07% of the 4,750,388 people in the mother-tongue table), followed by Hindko (3.79%), Urdu (1.8%).

Which province is Peshawar District in?

Peshawar District is in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, in Peshawar Division. Its headquarters is Peshawar.

What was the old name of Peshawar?

Purusapura. The present name, meaning frontier town, is ascribed to the Mughal emperor Akbar. (Encyclopaedia Britannica)
