Lahore District

Capital of Punjab · phone 042 · the city that taught the country how to be a city

Lahore is not a district you finish in one sitting. It is a city that has been practising being a capital for a thousand years and has never quite agreed which century it prefers. Walk from the Fort to a canal-road underpass in an afternoon and you will pass a Mughal tile, a Sikh samadhi, a British clock, a refugee shop opened in 1947, a university gate from the 1960s, and a housing society that did not exist when the last census enumerator was trained. Other districts in this atlas have a river, a shrine, a mill or a junction. Lahore has all of those and then a second city stacked on top, and a third. The beauty is not only the postcard — the Alamgiri Gate at dusk, the Badshahi courtyard after rain. The beauty is that the postcard is still a working neighbourhood. Someone is selling jalebi under the same brick that a Mughal mason signed with his thumb.

PBS 2023 puts about 13,004,135 people on 1,772 km². Density roughly 7,340 per km² — the thickest large sheet in Punjab. Households about two million. The whole district is counted urban. Men about 6.88 million, women about 6.12 million. Under-tens about 3.23 million. Literacy at age ten and above is 79.62 percent — 81.41 for men, 77.59 for women. That female row is high for Pakistan and still leaves more than one woman in five outside the written page. Official doors: lahore.punjab.gov.pk, and the Walled City Authority for the inner streets. Phone 042.

Punjabi is the mother tongue of about 9.55 million. Urdu about 2.74 million — the office, the campus, the refugee mohalla, the drama serial. Pashto about 268,000. Mewati about 261,000. Saraiki, Hindko and the rest are smaller rows. Lahore speaks Punjabi in the kitchen and Urdu on the news, and both on the same bus.

Ten tehsils of one city

Lahore is a district that ate its countryside. The old tehsil map has been recut into ten urban units.

Lahore City — about 214 km², 1.97 million people, literacy about 80.4 percent. The old core and its immediate ring.

Shalimar — about 272 km², 2.13 million, literacy about 81.2 percent. Gardens, Baghbanpura, the Mughal watercourse.

Model Town — about 353 km², 2.27 million, literacy about 78.9 percent. Planned twentieth-century suburb that became a city of its own.

Lahore Cantonment — about 466 km², 1.44 million, literacy about 81.0 percent. The British military grid and the quietest large roads.

Ravi — about 1.50 million. The river side and older industrial smear.

Iqbal Town — about 1.00 million. Mid-century planned housing that took the poet’s name.

Nishtar — about 973,000. A later cut of the southern city.

Raiwind — about 467 km², 738,000, literacy about 72.4 percent. The edge toward Kasur and the railway that still thinks it is leaving town.

Wagah — about 539,000. The border tehsil. Fields, new societies, the road that ends in flags.

Saddar — about 421,000. A slice of the older civil lines.

Fourteen National Assembly seats run across this one district. Lahore is a province inside a province.

A short history that is not short

Legend likes to give Lahore to Loh, a son of Rama, and to leave the proof in a temple story inside the Fort. Historians will not sign that page. What they will sign is older than the Mughals and younger than the river: a settlement on the Ravi that mattered enough for travellers to write the name down. Chinese pilgrims passed a city here. Al-Biruni knew it. By the later eleventh century Lahore was a Ghaznavid capital on the eastern edge of that empire — not yet the tile-and-marble city, but a city that knew paper, verse and a garrison.

The man who changed the inner weather of the city was not a king. Ali ibn Uthman al-Hujwiri, called Data Ganj Bakhsh, arrived from Ghazni in the eleventh century and died in Lahore. His book Kashf al-Mahjub is the oldest surviving Persian treatise on Sufism. His grave, just outside Bhati Gate, became the city’s other capital. Kings have come and gone through the thirteen gates. The Thursday night crowd at Data Darbar has outlasted most of them. If you want to know why Lahore feels different from a cantonment town laid out on a drawing board, start with that courtyard, not with a guidebook paragraph about Akbar.

The Delhi Sultanate used Lahore as a frontier and sometimes as a rival. Mongol raids burned it. It recovered because the river and the road to Delhi and to Kabul both wanted a city at this bend. Then the Mughals arrived and Lahore became, for stretches of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the other capital of an empire that also loved Agra and Delhi. Akbar made Lahore his headquarters for more than a decade, rebuilt the Fort in baked brick, and threw a wall around the city with gates that still organise the inner map. Jahangir is buried at Shahdara, across the Ravi. Nur Jahan lies nearby. Shah Jahan gave the city the Shalimar terraces and the marble rooms inside the Fort. Aurangzeb gave it the Badshahi Mosque — the courtyard that can hold tens of thousands and still feel like a single held breath when the prayer starts.

After the Mughals thinned, Lahore was fought over by Sikhs, Afghans and the wreckage of imperial governors. In 1799 Ranjit Singh took the city and made it the capital of a Punjabi kingdom. The Samadhi of Ranjit Singh stands beside the Badshahi Mosque as if the two buildings had agreed to share the same skyline. Hazuri Bagh is the garden between them. Gurdwara Dehra Sahib marks the place associated with Guru Arjan. The city that Muslims, Hindus and Sikhs had already shared for centuries became, under Ranjit Singh, a court that employed all three. That overlapping is the honest pre-Partition Lahore.

The British took the city after the Second Anglo-Sikh War, in 1849, and built a cantonment, a railway, a university, a museum, a High Court and wide civil lines. Government College opened in 1864. The University of the Punjab followed. Partition in 1947 cut the city more violently than any siege in the guidebooks. Hindu and Sikh Lahore left for the east. Muslim families from Amritsar, Jalandhar, Ludhiana, Delhi and farther south walked into empty houses. The language of the new mohallas was Urdu as often as Punjabi. That is why the 2023 table still shows more than two and a half million Urdu first-language speakers in a Punjabi capital.

After 1947 Lahore remained the intellectual capital of a country that put its official capital first in Karachi and then in Islamabad. The books, newspapers, courts, college arguments, film studios and Progressive Writers stayed here even when the ministries left. Since 1970 it has again been the capital of Punjab: Governor’s House, Assembly, High Court, secretariat, and the vanity of a city that knows the rest of the province must come to it for a file.

The Walled City and the thirteen gates

Androon Shehr is not a museum village. It is a living inner city of densest streets, balconies that almost touch, workshops on ground floors, and a skyline of satellite dishes above carved wood. Akbar’s wall once ran around it. Thirteen gates opened the brick.

Delhi Gate faces the old road to Delhi. Inside it the street becomes a bazaar of cloth, shoes and medical-supply shops. Wazir Khan Mosque sits on this approach — tiled from floor to the last leaf on the minaret, a 1630s prayer hall that is also a colour theory class.

Bhati Gate is the literary gate in local memory. Data Darbar is a short walk. Taxali Gate opened toward the old mint and the neighbourhood the city still discusses as Heera Mandi and as a food street. Roshnai Gate, the gate of lights, opens toward the Fort and the Badshahi Mosque. Kashmiri Gate pointed north. Lohari Gate took its name from the blacksmiths. Mochi Gate from the cobblers and from firework and kite shops. Shairanwala remembers lions from Ranjit Singh’s years. Masti, Mori, Yakki, Akbari and Shah Alam complete the old thirteen. Shah Alam burned in 1947. Akbari was taken down and not put back. Six gates still stand in some honest form. The rest survive as names on a bus.

The Walled City of Lahore Authority does the slow work: wires off a façade, a drain that does not sit on a Mughal plinth, a haveli that can take a visitor without throwing out the family who slept there last night. Beauty here is a boy carrying a tray of lassi under a carved balcony that has outlasted three governments.

Fort, mosque, garden

The Lahore Fort — Shahi Qila — is a small city of brick and marble. Akbar’s mass, Jahangir’s ornament, Shah Jahan’s marble, Sikh and British additions. Sheesh Mahal turns a lamp into a constellation. Naulakha Pavilion looks toward the Badshahi dome. Moti Masjid is the pearl chapel inside the walls. The Picture Wall on the north face is a tile-and-stone comic of elephants and court scenes. UNESCO listed the Fort together with Shalimar Gardens in 1981.

Step out of the Alamgiri Gate and the Badshahi Mosque fills the eye. Finished in the 1670s, the courtyard is a lake of red stone. On a Friday the crowd becomes a single geometry. Opposite, the Samadhi of Ranjit Singh keeps the other half of the same century. Between them Hazuri Bagh is a room of trees.

Shalimar is Shah Jahan’s three descending terraces: water, trees, pavilions. The terraces still work after rain. They work less well after a dry April. Lawrence Gardens — Bagh-e-Jinnah — is the British park that Lahore turned into a public living room. The canal bank at dusk is the city’s unofficial boulevard. Couples, vendors, the evening walk, the terrible traffic: Lahore being beautiful and ordinary at once.

The Mall, neighbourhoods that are cities

Outside the wall the British laid a second Lahore and called its spine the Mall. Museum, Town Hall, GPO, High Court, Cathedral, Government College’s red brick. The trees on the Mall are older than most of the ministries.

Defence, Gulberg, Model Town, Johar Town, Township, Iqbal Town, Faisal Town, Garden Town, Bahria, the societies along Raiwind Road — each is a city with a different evening. Johar Town and Township hold the middle-class majority the postcard never photographs. Youhanabad is one of the largest Christian neighbourhoods in Pakistan. Mughalpura’s workshops repaired the trains that made the junction cities of this atlas possible.

Minar-e-Pakistan stands in Iqbal Park on the ground where the 1940 resolution was passed. Go at late afternoon when the Fort and the Badshahi dome share the same light and the park is full of people who did not come for a civics lesson.

The Ravi, the fields, the border

The Ravi made Lahore possible and has been treated as a drain for fifty years. Older poems remember a river. Present photographs often show a channel. Shahdara’s tombs sit on the other bank. The fields toward Wagah grow wheat and houses in the same season. The Wagah-Attari border post is a theatre of flags every late afternoon. Ten kilometres back, a brick kiln and a new housing scheme share a dust cloud. That is also Lahore District.

Kitchens

Lahore’s reputation for food is a working habit. Breakfast can be nihari and kulcha, or Sunday halwa puri. Evening is charged karahi, paye, seekh, canal fish, winter sarson and makai, lassi thick enough to stand a spoon in, kulfi and falooda. Food Street at Fort Road looks at the Badshahi dome. Gowalmandi was the older night college of offal and talk. Anarkali is cloth, spice and the bookshop a student can still afford. Ichhra keeps a denser bazaar. Liberty and the malls are the air-conditioned cousins. A city that feeds thirteen million cannot put all of its flavour on one restored street for cameras.

Winter has Sargodha oranges on carts and peanuts on coal. Summer has the mango argument and the need for a room with a fan before anyone discusses architecture. Food here is how the city keeps its hours.

Music, verse, studios

If Data Darbar is one capital, the other is a page. Allama Iqbal taught and argued here; his tomb sits between the Badshahi Mosque and the Fort. Faiz Ahmed Faiz wrote here and went to jail from here. Nasir Kazmi’s Lahore is fog and absence. Intezar Hussain made the afterlife of Partition into a walking city of stories. Government College and the University of the Punjab are why a boy from Sargodha and a girl from Bahawalpur still take a bus to this district in order to become someone who reads.

Qawwali is Thursday at the Darbar, a wedding, a shrine urs. Film and television grew a whole industry here. National College of Arts teaches the next book cover and stage set. Alhamra on the Mall puts plays under one civic roof. The Lahore Museum holds the Fasting Buddha. Go back without the school checklist.

Four seasons in one bowl

Fog in January turns the canal into a suggestion. February wants kites and is not always allowed them. March is the first reliable sitting-outside. May and June are the test. Monsoon washes the Fort brick to a darker red and fills Shalimar’s channels. October is the best walking month. November and December are beauty and injury in the same week: rare clear mornings, and the closed-school weeks when the air is a medical fact. The people who love Lahore include the gardeners at Bagh-e-Jinnah and the men who still ice a block for a lassi shop inside Delhi Gate.

Festivals and the calendar

Urs of Data Ganj Bakhsh is the largest regular gathering in the city: langar, qawwali, rose and dust, faith as civic infrastructure.

Mela Chiraghan — the Festival of Lights — gathers at the shrine of Madho Lal Hussain, the sixteenth-century Sufi whose name is braided with Madho. Lahore still lights lamps for a poet who refused a single label.

Basant made Lahore famous for yellow cloth and rooftops, and infamous for chemical string. The honest paragraph holds both. A beautiful thing that kills people is still a beautiful thing and still a problem.

Eid turns the city into a visiting machine. Ashura processions move through old routes. Independence Day puts flags on the Canal and a crowd at Minar-e-Pakistan. Christmas is visible in Youhanabad. Vaisakhi brings Sikh pilgrims through Wagah. Diwali lamps still appear in the houses that remain. Smaller feasts: urs at Mian Mir, Bibi Pak Daman, the book fair, campus weeks on the canal.

People the city keeps

Data Ganj Bakhsh is the oldest resident who still receives visitors. Madho Lal Hussain is the poet of the lamps. Allama Iqbal, born in Sialkot, became a Lahore voice. Faiz Ahmed Faiz is the other national poet the city claims. Ranjit Singh made the Fort a Sikh court. Jahangir and Nur Jahan are buried across the river. Wazir Khan left a mosque instead of a memoir.

In the twentieth century the roll becomes a library. Manto wrote Partition from rooms that could have been these rooms. Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan made qawwali a world language from a Faisalabad birth and a Lahore stage. Noor Jehan was born in Kasur and became a Lahore microphone. Painters from NCA. Lawyers from the High Court bar. Abdus Salam taught here before the world prize. The point of a name on this page is not ownership. It is that Lahore was the room in which the work became public.

Books, presses, arguments

Lahore prints. The Urdu bazaar still sells the cheap edition a student can mark to death. Journals live and die here in a few rooms. The photocopy shop next to the college gate does more literacy work than a slogan. High Court corridors, Press Club tables, campus lawns, a shrine courtyard after the qawwali stops — Lahore talks. A capital that cannot argue becomes a secretariat. This one has not, yet, accepted that demotion.

Campuses and hospitals

University of the Punjab, GCU, UET on the canal, KEMU, UVAS, LUMS, NCA, and more private campuses than a paragraph should list. Lahore is why a student from Khushab or Khanewal or Kasur still leaves home.

Mayo, Services, Jinnah, Children’s Hospital, Punjab Institute of Cardiology, Sir Ganga Ram, Lady Willingdon, plus a private sector from modest nursing homes to Raiwind Road hospitals. A road accident on the GT Road still aims at Mayo. A paying patient may aim at the other end of the same city. The distance between those two doors is the health map.

Smog, heritage, speed

Winter air in Lahore is a civic emergency that repeats like a festival. Beauty does not cancel that. Heritage is the slower emergency: a haveli sold for a plaza, a cinema pulled down for parking. The Walled City work is the counter-argument. Thirteen million people will live somewhere, and the fields will lose. Metrobus, Orange Line, the choke at Kalma, the wagon that still does the work the map pretends a train does. Raiwind’s literacy row is the reminder that “Lahore” is not only the Mall.

How to see it without lying

Start at Roshnai Gate in the late afternoon. Walk the Fort wall. Sit in the Badshahi courtyard until the pigeons settle. Cross into the Walled City and lose your route on purpose. Come out at Delhi Gate and give Wazir Khan more time than the camera asks. The next morning, Shalimar before the heat. The evening, the canal. A Thursday, Data Darbar from the edge of the crowd. A February rooftop only if the string is honest. A border afternoon at Wagah if you need to remember that this district ends on a painted gate.

Then look at the census again. Thirteen million. Seventy-eight percent of women who can read. Two and a half million who first dreamed in Urdu. The beauty of Lahore is not that it is finished. The beauty is that it is still being argued over, still being cooked, still being prayed in, still being repaired with a budget that is always too small for a city this old and this many.

Lahore is the capital of Punjab and the memory of several capitals before that. It is a walled city that leaked into a metropolis. It is a garden that learned to live next to a flyover. It is a kitchen that can feed a wedding and a night shift from the same karahi. Other districts in this atlas have a single story that fits a heading. Lahore has a library. Walk it. Do not summarise it from a car.
