Sialkot

Gujranwala Division · code 052 · the export bench of Punjab

Sialkot is a workshop that learned to write poetry. The rest of Pakistan files it as Iqbal’s birthplace and then, in the next sentence, as the town that stitches the ball the World Cup rolls. Both sentences are true. Neither is the whole bench. Surgical steel, leather gloves, hockey sticks, a privately built airport, four tehsils of dense Punjabi villages, and a literacy row that looks like another century from Rojhan — that is the district when the camera has left the factory tour.

The 2023 census counted 4,499,394 people in about 671,000 households on 3,016 km². Urban 1.48 million. Rural 3.02 million. Literacy 78.37 percent — 80.24 among men, 76.50 among women. That women’s figure is among the strongest in Punjab. Punjabi first for about 4.12 million. Phone 052. Narowal walked out in 1991 and took Shakargarh with it. What stayed is still a heavy district.

Four tehsils. Sialkot — 2,088,201 people, literacy 79.42 percent. City, cantonment edge, Iqbal Manzil, the factory belt. Daska — 980,547, 79.19 percent. Pasrur — 970,366, 74.52 percent. Sambrial — 460,280, 79.89 percent, the strongest reader of the four.

A short history that is not only 1877

Sialkot is old in the way a Chenab-and-foothill town is old: a fort, a crossing, Sikh and Mughal layers, a British cantonment that bought sports kit from local workshops. The Uberoi brothers — Jhanda Singh and Ganda Singh — opened a racquet shop in the 1890s and then polo, cricket, hockey, football. Surgical work followed: scissors for private practitioners, then Sheffield steel, then a cluster. Partition emptied Hindu and Sikh factory offices. Muslim artisans who had been the hands became the owners. That transfer is the industrial fact of 1947 here, as real as the emptied mohallas of Rawalpindi, only with export orders attached.

The football, at the length it deserves

Sialkot does not field a team at the World Cup. It supplies the ball. That sentence should sit on the page without a joke. From the 1890s workshops the cluster learned leather. In the 1970s a Danish goalkeeper, Eigil Nielsen, worked with Anwar Khawaja and the Select line. In 1982 Sublime Sports in this city produced Adidas’s Tango España — the first official World Cup match ball made in Pakistan. Italy lifted that trophy in Spain. The stitch was Sialkot’s. Later tournaments kept coming back. Forward Sports and the Khawaja line have carried official Adidas match balls through recent Cups, including the 2026 tournament across the United States, Mexico and Canada. About six or seven of every ten footballs in the world still have a Sialkot beginning — match balls from factory lines, training and souvenir balls from more than a thousand registered stitching centres across the district.

The late-1990s child-labour scandal forced a monitoring system the industry still lives under: inspections, registered centres, a reputation the city cannot afford to lose twice. Hand-stitching is no longer the whole story. Machine-stitched and thermo-bonded match balls share the floor with the older 32-panel craft. A stitcher may finish a handful of match-quality balls in a day, more of the cheaper kind. World Cup years lift orders twenty or thirty percent. Sports goods as a whole earn hundreds of millions of dollars in exports; football is a slice of that, surgical instruments and leather the other slices. The city talks in containers, not in couplets, from Monday to Friday. Saturday it will still walk you to Iqbal Manzil.

This is not romance. Piece-rate pay, rush orders, a currency that moves, a European brand that prints its own name on the panel — the value chain is as unequal as any other export town. It is still the one Punjabi district whose product is touched, every four years, by the planet’s most watched final. Pakistan absent on the roster, present on the pitch. That is the civic boast. It is also a policy question: why a workshop this skilled still sends its children abroad for the degree the factory office needs.

Steel, leather, the airport

Surgical instruments are the quieter fame: forceps, scissors, a cluster that began before Partition and survived it. Gloves, bags, martial-arts kit, musical instruments in smaller sheds. Sialkot International Airport, built by the business community, is the first serious privately led civilian airport in the country — a fact that tells you how this chamber of commerce behaves when the state is slow. Dry port, expo, the road to the border. Daska and Sambrial feed the same export clock at a lower volume. Pasrur is more field, still literate.

People who belong

Allama Muhammad Iqbal was born here in 1877. Iqbal Manzil is the house the school trip already owns. Treat that as a birth-file fact, not a claim that the district invented the Pakistan idea by itself. Shoaib Malik is Sialkot’s cricket name — do not move him to Multan. Zaheer Abbas and other Test names sit on older lists from this belt. The factory houses — Khawaja, Sublime, Forward and the surgical families — are the industrial roll. Faiz Ahmed Faiz belongs to Kala Qader in today’s Narowal gazette; this page will not steal him back just because Narowal was once Sialkot. Chaudhry families and Kashmiri artisan lineages organise tickets and workshops both.

The street

Punjabi is the floor. A small Mewati row appears in the language table. Cantonment and city share a crowded plan. Chenab is the southern weather. The working week smells of glue and leather and autoclave steel. Sunday smells of Iqbal’s house and a marriage. Women’s literacy at 76 percent is the number that should travel with every World Cup caption. Same province as Liaquatpur. Different workshop.
