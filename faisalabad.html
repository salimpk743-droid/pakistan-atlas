Faisalabad District

Faisalabad Division · once Lyallpur · clock-tower city on the Chenab Colony canals · phone 041

Ask an older shopkeeper near the Clock Tower what the city is called and you will hear both names in the same sentence. Officially it has been Faisalabad since the late 1970s. In family talk, on bus sides, in the way people still say “Lyallpur Town” for one municipal slice of the map, the older name has not packed up and left. That double memory is not nostalgia for a colonial officer. It is how a planned canal town grew into Pakistan’s main textile workshop and never quite finished explaining itself.

The district around that tower is huge. The 2023 census put 9,075,819 people in 1,382,773 households on about 5,856 km². Density is roughly 1,550 people per square kilometre. Men 4,731,224, women 4,343,633. Urban and rural are almost level: about 4.39 million counted urban, about 4.68 million rural. Faisalabad City tehsil alone holds 3,691,999 people on only 168 km². That one tehsil is denser than most Pakistani districts. Literacy at age ten and above is 73.41 percent — 77.34 percent for men, 69.11 percent for women. City tehsil literacy is about 81.6 percent. Tandlianwala, out on the southern fields, is about 52.8 percent. The gap between those two rows is the real education map, not the university billboards on Jaranwala Road.

How a canal colony got a British name

Before the Lower Chenab Canal, this tract was not a city. It was a dry doab between the Chenab and the Ravi, administered in pieces from Jhang, Montgomery, Gujranwala and the rest. In the 1890s the canal colony opened the land. Settlers came on grants. A new headquarters was laid out in 1896 as a market town with eight bazaars running out from a central clock tower, like the Union Jack on a drawing board. The town and, from 1904, the district took the name Lyallpur after Sir James Broadwood Lyall, Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab when the colony work was being pushed through.

The plan was agricultural first. Wheat, cotton, sugarcane. Grain mandis. An agricultural college was approved in the first years of the new century; that campus is now the University of Agriculture, still the institution that makes more sense of this district than any textile slogan. Lyallpur district in those years also held what later became Toba Tek Singh. The railway tied the mandi to Karachi and to Lahore. By the 1910s the grain market was already crowded. The civil hospital grew with it. None of that was “industry” yet. Industry arrived when cotton from the same fields started staying in town to be ginned, spun and woven instead of leaving as a raw bale.

Why Lyallpur became Faisalabad

King Faisal bin Abdulaziz of Saudi Arabia was killed in March 1975. He had been a public friend of Pakistan in the years after 1971: aid, oil politics, the language of Muslim solidarity that the state wanted on the record. Pakistani cities looked for a way to put his name on the map. Lyallpur was the large industrial town that still carried a British lieutenant-governor in its title. On 1 September 1977 the federal government notified the change. The city and the district became Faisalabad.

Local memory, written down later by the Faisalabad writer Riaz Majid and repeated in Urdu accounts of the gazette, adds a small bureaucratic twist. The first revenue notification, the story goes, printed Shah Faisalabad. Three days later a second notification dropped “Shah” and left Faisalabad. Whether every clerk in 1977 remembered that sequence the same way is less important than the official fact: the name on the district board changed in 1977, in the first months of General Zia-ul-Haq’s government, as part of a wider tidying of colonial toponyms (Campbellpur became Attock in the same period).

Some English pages still print 1979. That date is when the new name had settled into common administrative use, and when municipal papers had caught up. The notification people in the city cite is September 1977. After 1982 the town was also a divisional headquarters, with Jhang and Toba Tek Singh under the same commissioner. Toba Tek Singh had already been cut away as its own district that year. The old Lyallpur district was no longer one piece of paper.

People did not stop saying Lyallpur overnight. Lyallpur Town remains a municipal label. GCUF still sits in a city whose cricket ground and older clubs remember the other name. The rename was a state decision about foreign friendship and post-colonial naming. It was not a vote in the eight bazaars. Both facts can sit on the same page.

The six tehsils now

Faisalabad City — 168 km², 3,691,999 people, literacy about 81.6 percent. This is the clock tower, D-Ground, Peoples Colony, Ghulam Muhammadabad, the Allied Hospital side, the university belt on Jail and Jaranwala roads. It is also the smog bowl on a still winter morning.

Faisalabad Sadar — 1,186 km², 1,742,958 people, literacy about 71.3 percent. The ring of canal villages and peri-urban chaks that are not quite the city and not quite Jaranwala. Khurrianwala, with its mills and the road toward the M-3, sits in this working belt more than in the old eight bazaars.

Jaranwala — 1,811 km², 1,731,148 people, literacy about 66.3 percent. Largest tehsil by land. A mandi town in its own right, sugar and grain, a THQ, and a long rural hinterland toward Nankana and Sheikhupura.

Samundri — 754 km², 729,672 people, literacy about 76.0 percent. Stronger school row than its size suggests. Cotton and cane country south of the city.

Tandlianwala — 1,284 km², 794,873 people, literacy about 52.8 percent. The weak literacy tehsil. More katcha edge, more distance from the city campuses.

Chak Jhumra — 654 km², 385,169 people, literacy about 70.6 percent. Smallest population. Railway and canal town on the Chiniot side, with its own THQ.

City-district papers also talk in “towns” — Iqbal, Jinnah, Madina, Lyallpur and the rest — from the 2005 local-government map. Those names are useful for municipal work. The census still walks tehsil by tehsil.

What the mills actually are

Call Faisalabad the Manchester of Pakistan and you have said something true and something lazy. The true part: this is where a large share of the country’s yarn, cloth, hosiery and home textiles is spun, dyed, stitched and packed. The district profile still lists figures that give the scale even when a unit or two has closed: more than a hundred spinning units, a few hundred processing and embroidery houses, tens of thousands of power looms, ready-made garment units, sizing and ginning, plus sugar, oil, soap, pumps and a long tail of small engineering shops. Power looms are not a footnote. They are the noisy room behind a lot of household income in the old city and in the chak settlements that feed the city.

The lazy part is to pretend the clock tower is a factory gate. Most of the heavy spinning and processing sat, and still sits, on Sargodha Road, Jaranwala Road, Khurrianwala, the small industrial estates, and inside unmarked streets where a loom shed shares a wall with a house. Export houses use the dry port and the road to Port Qasim. A shirt sold in Europe may have been cut in a unit that does not have its name on the building. Labour in those rooms is the other half of the “mill city” story: piece rates, long shifts, cotton dust, and a union map that has never been as tidy as the eight bazaars.

Agriculture did not leave when the mills arrived. Wheat, sugarcane, cotton, maize, fodder. Guava and citrus on the better plots. The University of Agriculture and NIAB / AARI research farms are still here because the colony was a farm project first. A good cane year and a bad yarn year can hit the same household if one brother is on a loom and the other is on a square of canal land in Sadar.

Money that does not all sit in one bazaar

Grain and cloth mandis around the Clock Tower still set a morning price. D-Ground and the newer commercial strips take the retail and the banks. Khurrianwala takes a lot of the actual manufacturing traffic. The M-3 and M-4 put the city on the Lahore–Multan motorway system; Faisalabad International Airport takes the passenger load that used to mean a drive to Lahore. None of that removes the inner-city congestion or the truck queue on a mill road at dusk.

Chiniot furniture, Jhang cane and Toba cotton all pass through this division. Faisalabad is the workshop that keeps them. It is also the place students from those districts come for a medical seat or an engineering class they cannot find at home. That pull is why Allied Hospital corridors do not look like a district hospital in Bhowana.

Who speaks what

Punjabi is the mother tongue of about 8.53 million people on the 2023 table — well over ninety percent. Urdu about 407,000. Pashto about 58,000. Saraiki about 42,000. This is a central-Punjab industrial district, not a Derajat town. The Punjabi heard in a loom shed and the Urdu heard in a college office are both local. English is a campus and export-office language.

Campuses that grew out of the colony college

University of Agriculture Faisalabad is the old backbone. Government College University Faisalabad grew out of the government college. National Textile University is the campus that matches the mills on paper. University of Faisalabad, Punjab Medical College / Faisalabad Medical University, a UET presence, and a long list of public and private colleges fill the city and the tehsil towns. Government school stock in the district profile runs past two thousand campuses. That stock is not evenly good. City tehsil can show high matric numbers while a Tandlianwala girls’ primary still waits on a teacher posting. Students who can pay still leave for Lahore. Students who cannot, work the loom and sit an evening class if the shift allows.

Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan was from this city. The government general hospital that carries his name is on the municipal list. That is a local fact, not a tourist brochure.

Allied Hospital and the tehsil line

Allied Hospital, Allied-II, Faisalabad Institute of Cardiology, the Children Hospital and the teaching hospital at Ghulam Muhammadabad are the public tertiary cluster. Government General Hospital Samanabad and the Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan hospital add city beds. THQ hospitals sit in Jaranwala, Samundri, Tandlianwala and Chak Jhumra. Khurrianwala has a smaller public hospital. Fourteen RHCs and more than a hundred BHUs are the rural net. PINUM and a string of private houses take oncology and paying patients. A loom injury at night still depends on which road is open and which ward has a surgeon.

Air, water, shifts

Winter smog in Faisalabad is not a metaphor. Brick kilns, loom exhaust, canal dust and the same fog that sits on Lahore collect over a bowl of factories and chaks. Summer heat on a tin loom shed is the other season. Drain water from dyeing units has been an old complaint on the channels that leave the city. None of this is solved by renaming a road after a king.

Labour is the quiet civic issue. Power-loom households run on orders that can stop in a week. Child labour in small units is not unknown. Fire in a packed shed is a city news item every few years. The district that exports bedsheets also exports tired lungs. A serious atlas page has to hold both.

Crowding in City tehsil is the planning issue. 22,000 people per square kilometre on paper means shared rooms, shared taps, and schools on double shift. Sadar and Jaranwala take the spillover. New housing schemes sell a cleaner air that the motorway does not always deliver.

A short walk around the names

Clock Tower and the eight bazaars. D-Ground. Ghulam Muhammadabad. Peoples Colony. Sargodha Road mills. Jaranwala Road campuses. Khurrianwala. Chak Jhumra station. Samundri mandi. Tandlianwala town. Dijkot. The old agricultural college farms. Chenab College is in Chiniot; do not mix the two river towns.

Faisalabad is still the colony town that learned to spin. The lieutenant-governor’s name came off the board in 1977 because a Saudi king had been killed and a Pakistani government wanted that friendship carved into a district title. The looms did not care what the board said. They still do not. The useful questions in 2026 are the same ones the agricultural college was founded to ask, only louder: who owns the water, who breathes the winter air, and which child in Tandlianwala gets as far as the city campus.
