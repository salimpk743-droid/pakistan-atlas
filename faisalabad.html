Faisalabad District

Faisalabad Division · once Lyallpur · clock-tower city on the Chenab Colony canals · phone 041

Faisalabad District: Quick Answers

Faisalabad District is one of the 41 districts of Punjab, in Faisalabad Division, with its headquarters at Faisalabad. It has 6 tehsils: Chak Jhumra, Faisalabad City, Faisalabad Sadar, Jaranwala, Sammundri and Tandlian Wala. The 2023 census counted 9,075,819 people on 5,857 km², and 73.41% of residents aged 10 and over were literate.

Ask an older shopkeeper near the Clock Tower what the city is called and you will hear both names in the same sentence. Officially it has been Faisalabad since the late 1970s. In family talk, on bus sides, in the way people still say “Lyallpur Town” for one municipal slice of the map, the older name has not packed up and left. That double memory is not nostalgia for a colonial officer. It is how a planned canal town grew into Pakistan’s main textile workshop and never quite finished explaining itself.

The district around that tower is huge. The 2023 census put 9,075,819 people in 1,382,773 households on about 5,856 km². Density is roughly 1,550 people per square kilometre. Men 4,731,224, women 4,343,633. Urban and rural are almost level: about 4.39 million counted urban, about 4.68 million rural. Faisalabad City tehsil alone holds 3,691,999 people on only 168 km². That one tehsil is denser than most Pakistani districts. Literacy at age ten and above is 73.41 percent — 77.34 percent for men, 69.11 percent for women. City tehsil literacy is about 81.6 percent. Tandlianwala, out on the southern fields, is about 52.8 percent. The gap between those two rows is the real education map, not the university billboards on Jaranwala Road.

Earlier counts help show the climb. The tract that became this district held a little over three million people in 1972, about 3.56 million in 1981, 5.43 million in 1998, 7.88 million in 2017. The jump is mills plus canal land plus the way a second city in Punjab pulls workers from Jhang, Toba, Chiniot, Sahiwal and farther. Faisalabad Division as a whole, with those neighbours, was about 16.2 million in 2023. The city is the workshop of that division, not a suburb of Lahore, even if Lahore still takes the weekend shopping and the specialist degree.

How a canal colony got a British name

Before the Lower Chenab Canal, this tract was not a city. It was a dry doab between the Chenab and the Ravi, administered in pieces from Jhang, Montgomery, Gujranwala and the rest. In the 1890s the canal colony opened the land. Settlers came on grants — peasant grantees, yeomen, a smaller layer of capitalist farms. A new headquarters was laid out in 1896 as a market town with eight bazaars running out from a central clock tower, like the Union Jack on a drawing board. Katchery Bazaar, Rail Bazaar, Chiniot Bazaar, Jhang Bazaar and the others still follow those spokes. The town and, from 1904, the district took the name Lyallpur after Sir James Broadwood Lyall, Lieutenant-Governor of the Punjab when the colony work was being pushed through.

The plan was agricultural first. Wheat, cotton, sugarcane. Grain mandis. An agricultural college was approved in the first years of the new century; that campus is now the University of Agriculture, still the institution that makes more sense of this district than any textile slogan. Lyallpur district in those years also held what later became Toba Tek Singh. The railway tied the mandi to Karachi and to Lahore. By the 1910s the grain market was already crowded. The civil hospital grew with it. None of that was “industry” yet. Industry arrived when cotton from the same fields started staying in town to be ginned, spun and woven instead of leaving as a raw bale.

Partition in 1947 changed the streets more than the canals. Hindu and Sikh trading families left. Muslim refugees from the eastern Punjab and from farther afield took shops in the same eight bazaars. The colony grid survived. The people inside it did not stay the same. A lot of the later mill money was made by families who arrived with a suitcase and learned the loom or the yarn commission shop. That is ordinary Faisalabad history, not a special myth.

Why Lyallpur became Faisalabad

King Faisal bin Abdulaziz of Saudi Arabia was killed in March 1975. He had been a public friend of Pakistan in the years after 1971: aid, oil politics, the language of Muslim solidarity that the state wanted on the record. Pakistani cities looked for a way to put his name on the map. Lyallpur was the large industrial town that still carried a British lieutenant-governor in its title. On 1 September 1977 the federal government notified the change. The city and the district became Faisalabad.

Local memory, written down later by the Faisalabad writer Riaz Majid and repeated in Urdu accounts of the gazette, adds a small bureaucratic twist. The first revenue notification, the story goes, printed Shah Faisalabad. Three days later a second notification dropped “Shah” and left Faisalabad. Whether every clerk in 1977 remembered that sequence the same way is less important than the official fact: the name on the district board changed in 1977, in the first months of General Zia-ul-Haq’s government, as part of a wider tidying of colonial toponyms. Campbellpur became Attock in the same stretch of years. Montgomery had already become Sahiwal. The pattern was clear even if the paperwork in each deputy commissioner’s office was messy.

Some English pages still print 1979. That date is when the new name had settled into common administrative use, and when municipal papers had caught up. The notification people in the city cite is September 1977. After 1982 the town was also a divisional headquarters, with Jhang and Toba Tek Singh under the same commissioner. Toba Tek Singh had already been cut away as its own district that year. The old Lyallpur district was no longer one piece of paper.

People did not stop saying Lyallpur overnight. Lyallpur Town remains a municipal label. GCUF still sits in a city whose cricket ground and older clubs remember the other name. Some export letterheads kept Lyallpur in small type for years because a buyer in Manchester already knew that word. The rename was a state decision about foreign friendship and post-colonial naming. It was not a vote in the eight bazaars. Both facts can sit on the same page.

The six tehsils now

Faisalabad City — 168 km², 3,691,999 people, literacy about 81.6 percent. This is the clock tower, D-Ground, Peoples Colony, Ghulam Muhammadabad, the Allied Hospital side, the university belt on Jail and Jaranwala roads. It is also the smog bowl on a still winter morning.

Faisalabad Sadar — 1,186 km², 1,742,958 people, literacy about 71.3 percent. The ring of canal villages and peri-urban chaks that are not quite the city and not quite Jaranwala. Khurrianwala, with its mills and the road toward the M-3, sits in this working belt more than in the old eight bazaars. Dijkot is another name on this ring.

Jaranwala — 1,811 km², 1,731,148 people, literacy about 66.3 percent. Largest tehsil by land. A mandi town in its own right, sugar and grain, a THQ, and a long rural hinterland toward Nankana and Sheikhupura. Jaranwala has its own civic fights and its own flood-and-drain years; it is not a suburb with a different postcode.

Samundri — 754 km², 729,672 people, literacy about 76.0 percent. Stronger school row than its size suggests. Cotton and cane country south of the city.

Tandlianwala — 1,284 km², 794,873 people, literacy about 52.8 percent. The weak literacy tehsil. More katcha edge, more distance from the city campuses. If you only read city billboards you will miss this tehsil entirely.

Chak Jhumra — 654 km², 385,169 people, literacy about 70.6 percent. Smallest population. Railway and canal town on the Chiniot side, with its own THQ.

City-district papers also talk in “towns” — Iqbal, Jinnah, Madina, Lyallpur and the rest — from the 2005 local-government map. Those names are useful for municipal work. The census still walks tehsil by tehsil.

What the mills actually are

Call Faisalabad the Manchester of Pakistan and you have said something true and something lazy. The true part: this is where a large share of the country’s yarn, cloth, hosiery and home textiles is spun, dyed, stitched and packed. The district profile still lists figures that give the scale even when a unit or two has closed: more than a hundred spinning units, a few hundred processing and embroidery houses, tens of thousands of power looms, ready-made garment units, sizing and ginning, plus sugar, oil, soap, pumps and a long tail of small engineering shops. Power looms are not a footnote. They are the noisy room behind a lot of household income in the old city and in the chak settlements that feed the city.

The lazy part is to pretend the clock tower is a factory gate. Most of the heavy spinning and processing sat, and still sits, on Sargodha Road, Jaranwala Road, Khurrianwala, the small industrial estates, and inside unmarked streets where a loom shed shares a wall with a house. Export houses use Faisalabad Dry Port and the road-and-rail link toward Port Qasim. A shirt sold in Europe may have been cut in a unit that does not have its name on the building. Commission agents in the old cloth mandi still make a living matching a mill to an order. That trade is as old as the colony grain pit, only the commodity changed.

Labour in those rooms is the other half of the mill-city story: piece rates, long shifts, cotton dust, and a union map that has never been as tidy as the eight bazaars. When an order stops, a household that bought a loom on credit feels it before the export house does. When an order floods in, the same household runs two shifts and pulls a nephew out of school for a month. That is not a metaphor. Teachers in the inner city will tell you the same pattern without being asked.

Agriculture did not leave when the mills arrived. Wheat, sugarcane, cotton, maize, fodder. Guava and citrus on the better plots. The University of Agriculture and the NIAB and AARI research farms are still here because the colony was a farm project first. A good cane year and a bad yarn year can hit the same household if one brother is on a loom and the other is on a square of canal land in Sadar. Sugar mills on the district list are few; they still set a crushing-season rhythm in Jaranwala and Samundri that the city barely notices.

Money that does not all sit in one bazaar

Grain and cloth mandis around the Clock Tower still set a morning price. D-Ground and the newer commercial strips take the retail and the banks. Khurrianwala takes a lot of the actual manufacturing traffic. The M-3 and M-4 put the city on the Lahore–Multan motorway system. Faisalabad International Airport takes the passenger load that used to mean a drive to Lahore. None of that removes the inner-city congestion or the truck queue on a mill road at dusk.

Chiniot furniture, Jhang cane and Toba cotton all pass through this division. Faisalabad is the workshop that keeps them. It is also the place students from those districts come for a medical seat or an engineering class they cannot find at home. That pull is why Allied Hospital corridors do not look like a district hospital in Bhowana. A private college on Jaranwala Road may be teaching a boy from Lalian and a girl from Gojra in the same room. The fee slip does not record that geography. The bus stand does.

Small engineering — pumps, machine parts, loom spare parts — is the quiet cousin of the textile story. A broken loom in a back street is often repaired before sunset because someone within a kilometre makes the part. That density of workshops is what people mean, whether they know it or not, when they call this a business city. It is not only listed companies. It is the spare-part shop that never took out an advertisement.

Who speaks what

Punjabi is the mother tongue of about 8.53 million people on the 2023 table — well over ninety percent. Urdu about 407,000. Pashto about 58,000. Saraiki about 42,000. This is a central-Punjab industrial district, not a Derajat town. The Punjabi heard in a loom shed and the Urdu heard in a college office are both local. English is a campus and export-office language. Migrants from the hills and from the south add Pashto and Saraiki rows that stay small on the district sheet and visible on a few streets.

Campuses that grew out of the colony college

University of Agriculture Faisalabad is the old backbone. Walk that campus in exam week and you will see students from half of Punjab, not only from the eight bazaars. Government College University Faisalabad grew out of the government college. National Textile University is the campus that matches the mills on paper; its graduates are supposed to go into the sheds, though many go into offices that never touch a loom. University of Faisalabad, Punjab Medical College / Faisalabad Medical University, a UET presence, and a long list of public and private colleges fill the city and the tehsil towns.

Government school stock in the district profile runs past two thousand campuses. That stock is not evenly good. City tehsil can show high matric numbers while a Tandlianwala girls’ primary still waits on a teacher posting. Students who can pay still leave for Lahore. Students who cannot, work the loom and sit an evening class if the shift allows. The literacy gap between City tehsil and Tandlianwala is the education fact that survives every ribbon-cutting.

Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan was from this city. The government general hospital that carries his name is on the municipal list. That is a local fact, not a tourist brochure.

Allied Hospital and the tehsil line

Allied Hospital, Allied-II, Faisalabad Institute of Cardiology, the Children Hospital and the teaching hospital at Ghulam Muhammadabad are the public tertiary cluster. Government General Hospital Samanabad and the Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan hospital add city beds. THQ hospitals sit in Jaranwala, Samundri, Tandlianwala and Chak Jhumra. Khurrianwala has a smaller public hospital. Fourteen RHCs and more than a hundred BHUs are the rural net. PINUM and a string of private houses take oncology and paying patients. A loom injury at night still depends on which road is open and which ward has a surgeon. Cardiac cases from Jhang and Toba still come here because FIC is the regional name.

Air, water, shifts

Winter smog in Faisalabad is not a metaphor. Brick kilns, loom exhaust, canal dust and the same fog that sits on Lahore collect over a bowl of factories and chaks. Schools close for a day and reopen as if the air had learned a lesson. Summer heat on a tin loom shed is the other season. Drain water from dyeing units has been an old complaint on the channels that leave the city. None of this is solved by renaming a road after a king.

Labour is the quiet civic issue. Power-loom households run on orders that can stop in a week. Child labour in small units is not unknown. Fire in a packed shed is a city news item every few years. The district that exports bedsheets also exports tired lungs. Factory inspections exist on paper. The shed behind a house is harder to inspect than a listed spinning mill with a signboard.

Crowding in City tehsil is the planning issue. Twenty-two thousand people per square kilometre on paper means shared rooms, shared taps, and schools on double shift. Sadar and Jaranwala take the spillover. New housing schemes sell a cleaner air that the motorway does not always deliver. Water table and the old colony minors are under the same pressure as the air. The agricultural college was founded to think about water. The city has grown past the problem the college was sized for.

A short walk around the names

Clock Tower and the eight bazaars. D-Ground. Ghulam Muhammadabad. Peoples Colony. Samanabad. Sargodha Road mills. Jaranwala Road campuses. Khurrianwala. Chak Jhumra station. Samundri mandi. Tandlianwala town. Dijkot. The old agricultural college farms. Chenab College is in Chiniot; do not mix the two river towns.

Faisalabad is still the colony town that learned to spin. The lieutenant-governor’s name came off the board in 1977 because a Saudi king had been killed and a Pakistani government wanted that friendship carved into a district title. The looms did not care what the board said. They still do not. The useful questions in 2026 are the same ones the agricultural college was founded to ask, only louder: who owns the water, who breathes the winter air, and which child in Tandlianwala gets as far as the city campus.

Faisalabad District: FAQ

How many tehsils are in Faisalabad District?

Faisalabad District has 6 tehsils: Chak Jhumra, Faisalabad City, Faisalabad Sadar, Jaranwala, Sammundri and Tandlian Wala.

What is the population of Faisalabad District?

The 2023 census counted 9,075,819 people in Faisalabad District, up from 7,882,444 in 2017 (annual growth 2.38%). 48.4% of the population lived in urban areas.

What is the area of Faisalabad District?

Faisalabad District covers 5,857 km² according to the 2023 census, a density of 1,549.57 people per km².

What is the literacy rate of Faisalabad District?

73.41% of people aged 10 and over were literate in the 2023 census (male 77.34%, female 69.11%).

Which languages are spoken in Faisalabad District?

The most common mother tongue in the 2023 census was Punjabi (94.13% of the 9,065,306 people in the mother-tongue table), followed by Urdu (4.49%).

Which province is Faisalabad District in?

Faisalabad District is in Punjab, in Faisalabad Division. Its headquarters is Faisalabad.

What was the old name of Faisalabad?

Lyallpur, after Sir Charles James Lyall, lieutenant governor of the Punjab when the city was founded in 1890. (Encyclopaedia Britannica)
