Pakistan's Political History

1947 — 2026

A documentary archive of how Pakistan’s political system developed from independence through the verified record of 2026: constitutions, parliament, elections, governments, parties, provinces, military-led and civilian periods, and the crises that reshaped the state.

Home · Political history archive

Pakistan was created on 14 August 1947 as a dominion in the Commonwealth, with a Governor-General and a Prime Minister, and with the Government of India Act 1935 as its interim constitutional framework. The political history that followed is not a single story of democracy or of military rule. It is a record of constitution-making and constitution-breaking; of parliamentary governments, presidential experiments, martial law, caretaker administrations and judicial interventions; of a two-wing federation that ended in 1971; and of a parliamentary republic that, after 2008, completed successive elected transfers even as the civil–military balance and the courts remained central to politics. This page is a collective archive, not a party narrative. Disputed events are attributed. Dates of office follow the National Assembly’s official lists unless a discrepancy is noted.

Foundation of the state

1947 — The New State

At midnight on 14–15 August 1947 the British Indian Empire was partitioned. Pakistan came into existence as a dominion under the Indian Independence Act 1947. The new state had two geographically separated wings, East Bengal (later East Pakistan) and a western grouping of provinces and princely-state territories, with a thousand miles of Indian territory between them. Muhammad Ali Jinnah became the first Governor-General. He was not President of Pakistan: that office did not exist until the 1956 Constitution. Jinnah was also the first President of the Constituent Assembly. Liaquat Ali Khan, leader of the Pakistan Muslim League in the Assembly, became the first Prime Minister on 15 August 1947, the date recorded by the National Assembly’s official list.

The immediate constitutional problem was that Pakistan had a state but not a finished constitution. Until a constituent assembly could write one, the Government of India Act 1935, as adapted by the Independence Act and subsequent orders, served as the interim framework. That Act had been designed for a federal British India. It gave the centre wide emergency and residual powers and left the new government to improvise a federation out of provinces, princely states and tribal areas whose accession and administrative integration were still incomplete.

The administrative crisis was as severe as the constitutional one. Partition produced one of the largest forced migrations of the twentieth century. Refugee rehabilitation, the division of military and civil assets, the establishment of a central secretariat (first in Karachi), and the need to staff a new civil service all competed with constitution-making. Several princely states acceded to Pakistan, among them Bahawalpur, Khairpur, Kalat (after a disputed interval in 1947–48), and the frontier states. Junagadh’s accession was not made effective. The dispute over Jammu and Kashmir, after the ruler’s accession to India and the first India–Pakistan war of 1947–48, became a lasting feature of Pakistan’s foreign and domestic politics. A United Nations-supervised ceasefire line took shape in 1949; the political consequence inside Pakistan was an early fusion of national security, military influence and Kashmir policy.

The early federal structure was asymmetric. East Bengal contained the majority of the population. West Pakistan contained the capital, the larger share of the civil-military elite, and the army’s main recruiting grounds. That imbalance — population in the east, power in the west — was the central unsolved problem of the first twenty-four years. Jinnah died in Karachi on 11 September 1948. Khawaja Nazimuddin succeeded him as Governor-General. Liaquat Ali Khan was assassinated in Rawalpindi on 16 October 1951. Nazimuddin then became Prime Minister, and Ghulam Muhammad, the finance minister, became Governor-General. Those two successions moved the centre of gravity from the League’s political leadership toward the bureaucratic executive — a shift that shaped the 1950s.

The Objectives Resolution, 1949

On 7 March 1949 Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan introduced the Objectives Resolution in the Constituent Assembly. The Assembly adopted it on 12 March 1949. The Resolution set out the aims that were to guide the future constitution: sovereignty as a trust from God, to be exercised by the people through chosen representatives; democracy, freedom, equality and social justice “as enunciated by Islam”; adequate provision for Muslims to order their lives according to the Quran and Sunnah; and, equally, the safeguarding of the legitimate interests of minorities, including their freedom to practise their religions and develop their cultures.

The Resolution was at first a preamble — a statement of intent, not an operative clause that courts could enforce against the rest of the constitution. Its later career is therefore a constitutional story in its own right. When the 1973 Constitution was revived in 1985, the Eighth Amendment inserted the Objectives Resolution into the Constitution as Article 2A, making it a substantive part of the text. Supporters have treated Article 2A as the moral and Islamic foundation of the republic. Critics have argued that converting a preamble into an operative article created a hierarchy of clauses and opened the door to contested judicial interpretation of what “Islamisation” required. Both readings belong in the historical record; the legal fact is the insertion of Article 2A in 1985.

Offices, not titles. Jinnah was Governor-General of the Dominion of Pakistan and President of the Constituent Assembly. Liaquat Ali Khan was Prime Minister. Iskander Mirza became the first President only when the 1956 Constitution came into force on 23 March 1956. This archive keeps those offices distinct.

Governor-Generals, 1947–1956

The office of Governor-General existed only while Pakistan was a dominion, from independence until 23 March 1956. The Governor-General represented the Crown and, in practice, became a powerful head of state — especially after 1951, when Ghulam Muhammad used the office to dismiss a prime minister and, in 1954, to dissolve the first Constituent Assembly.

Constitutional history

Constitutions & Institutions

Pakistan has had three constitutions (1956, 1962, 1973), one long interim use of a colonial statute (1947–1956), an interim constitution (1972), and repeated extra-constitutional interruptions. The 1973 Constitution remains in force. It has been suspended, revived, and heavily amended. The National Assembly currently publishes the text “as amended up to 21 November 2025,” which includes the Twenty-sixth Amendment (2024) and the Twenty-seventh Amendment (2025).

The 1956 Constitution

After nine years of drafting, the first Constituent Assembly was dissolved by Governor-General Ghulam Muhammad in October 1954 — a crisis litigated in the Maulvi Tamizuddin Khan case. A second Constituent Assembly produced the 1956 Constitution. Pakistan ceased to be a dominion and became the Islamic Republic of Pakistan. The system was parliamentary: a President as head of state and a Prime Minister as head of government, responsible to a unicameral National Assembly. One Unit, imposed in 1955, had already merged the western provinces into a single West Pakistan to create a parity of two units — East and West — in the federal legislature. That parity, not population, was the political price of constitution-making. The document lasted two and a half years.

The 1962 Constitution

Ayub Khan’s constitution replaced parliamentary government with a presidential system. The President was the chief executive. The legislature was unicameral. Members were elected indirectly by an electoral college of “Basic Democrats” — local councillors created under the Basic Democracies Order of 1959. The arrangement was presented as controlled democracy; opponents treated it as a method of insulating the presidency from adult-franchise politics. Fatima Jinnah contested the 1965 presidential election against Ayub; official results returned Ayub. Urban protest in 1968–69 ended the system. Ayub resigned on 25 March 1969 and handed power to General Yahya Khan.

The 1973 Constitution

The 1973 Constitution is Pakistan’s longest-lived basic law. It established a parliamentary federal republic with a bicameral parliament: a National Assembly elected by adult franchise on a population basis, and a Senate giving the provinces more equal representation. The Prime Minister is head of government; the President is head of state. The original 1973 design made the Prime Minister the effective centre of executive power. Later amendments moved that centre toward the President (Eighth and Seventeenth) and then back toward parliament (Thirteenth and Eighteenth). Islamic provisions, fundamental rights, a federal legislative list, and provincial autonomy are all part of the same text, which is why amendment politics has been so intense.

How to read the presidency. Pakistan’s President has not always held the same powers. Under 1956 and the original 1973 text the office was largely ceremonial in design. Under 1962, and again under Zia (Eighth Amendment) and Musharraf (Seventeenth), the President could dissolve the National Assembly. The Eighteenth Amendment (2010) returned the parliamentary centre of gravity. Treat each presidency as a constitutional period, not a single office with a fixed meaning.

Civil–military sequence

Military Rule & Civilian Rule

Pakistan’s political calendar is often described as an alternation of “civilian” and “military” periods. The record is more precise than that. There have been martial-law governments, military presidents ruling through a constitution they themselves issued, civilian presidents with the power to dismiss elected cabinets, caretaker cabinets appointed to hold elections, and parliamentary governments with and without a working majority. The labels below follow offices and proclamations, not moral verdicts.

1947–58 parliamentary / bureaucratic 1958–71 military-led 1971–77 civilian 1977–88 military-led 1988–99 elected, interrupted 1999–08 military-led

After 2008 the National Assembly completed its term (2008–13) and transferred power to another elected government (2013–18), then to a third (2018–22). That sequence is unique in the country’s history. It did not end civil–military or judicial-political conflict; it did change the baseline from extra-constitutional takeover to parliamentary and legal contest.

1958: first Martial Law

On 7 October 1958 President Iskander Mirza abrogated the 1956 Constitution, dismissed the government of Feroz Khan Noon, dissolved legislatures and proclaimed Martial Law, appointing General Mohammad Ayub Khan as Chief Martial Law Administrator. Twenty days later, on 27 October 1958, Ayub Khan removed Mirza and assumed the presidency. The Supreme Court’s Dosso judgment (1958) treated the revolution as a successful law-creating fact — a doctrine later rejected in Asma Jilani (1972). Ayub combined military presidency with Basic Democracies, a 1962 presidential constitution, and a development-state rhetoric. The 1965 war with India and the 1968–69 urban movement ended the experiment. Ayub resigned on 25 March 1969. General Agha Mohammad Yahya Khan imposed a second Martial Law.

The 1970 general election

The Legal Framework Order, 1970, issued by Yahya Khan, promised the first general election in Pakistan’s history on the basis of adult franchise and population, and dissolved One Unit so that the four western provinces reappeared. National Assembly polling was held on 7 December 1970; provincial polling followed on 17 December. These were not a continuation of any earlier “nationwide general election”: from 1947 to 1970 Pakistan had not held a National Assembly election on adult franchise.

The result produced a dual mandate. The Awami League, led by Sheikh Mujibur Rahman and contesting on the 1966 Six-Point programme of provincial autonomy, won 160 of the 162 National Assembly seats allocated to East Pakistan and therefore an overall majority of the 300 general seats. The Pakistan Peoples Party, led by Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, won 81 seats, concentrated in the Punjab and Sindh. No party spanned both wings. The constitutional question was whether power would be transferred to the majority party, and on what terms a constitution would then be written.

Negotiations in Dhaka and Islamabad in early 1971 failed. The National Assembly session scheduled for 3 March was postponed. Political confrontation in East Pakistan widened into civil conflict after the military operation that began in Dhaka on 25 March 1971. A government-in-exile of Bangladesh was proclaimed. In December 1971 the civil conflict became an India–Pakistan war. Pakistani forces in the east surrendered on 16 December 1971. East Pakistan became the independent state of Bangladesh. The political consequence for the remaining Pakistan was a legitimacy crisis, the end of the two-wing federation, and the transfer of power to Zulfikar Ali Bhutto on 20 December 1971 as President and Chief Martial Law Administrator. Nurul Amin had been appointed Prime Minister on 7 December 1971 for thirteen days; the National Assembly records his tenure as 7–20 December 1971.

Language on 1971 is kept deliberately exact. There was a constitutional crisis after the 1970 election; a civil conflict in East Pakistan after 25 March 1971; a military operation by the Pakistan Army; and an international war with India in December 1971. Competing national narratives exist in Pakistan, Bangladesh and India. This archive records the sequence of political facts, not a wartime verdict.

East Pakistan and 1971

The two-wing structure was never only a map. East Bengal / East Pakistan had the larger population and, after 1955, was one of two units in a parity federation that denied it a population majority in the legislature. The 1952 language movement in Dhaka, the 1954 provincial election victory of the United Front over the Muslim League, the dismissal of that ministry, and the 1966 Six Points were successive political expressions of the same grievance: representation, language, and the location of power. The 1970 election converted that grievance into a parliamentary majority that was not allowed to form a government. After 1971 the remaining Pakistan rewrote its territory (First Amendment, 1974), its constitution (1973), and its idea of the federation. The shock remains a founding fact of the post-1971 political system.

Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, 1971–1977

Bhutto assumed office on 20 December 1971 as President and Chief Martial Law Administrator. An Interim Constitution followed in 1972. Martial law was lifted as the 1973 Constitution came into force on 14 August 1973; Bhutto stepped down from the presidency to become Prime Minister (National Assembly list: 14 August 1973 – 5 July 1977). Fazal Elahi Chaudhry became the civilian President. The new constitution was federal and parliamentary, and was adopted with the support of opposition parties then in the Assembly.

The Bhutto government nationalised major industries, banks and educational institutions; hosted the Islamic Summit in Lahore in 1974; and signed the Simla Agreement with India in 1972, which converted the 1971 ceasefire line in Kashmir into the Line of Control and committed both states to bilateral negotiation. A nuclear programme was pursued after India’s 1974 test; later governments continued it. The Second Amendment (1974) altered the constitutional status of Ahmadis. Opposition coalesced in the provinces — notably in Balochistan and the NWFP, where the National Awami Party governments were dismissed in 1973 — and then nationally in the Pakistan National Alliance (PNA) before the 1977 election.

National Assembly polling was held on 7 March 1977. Official results gave the PPP a large majority. The PNA alleged widespread rigging and launched a mass movement. Negotiations for a re-election were under way when, on 5 July 1977, General Mohammad Zia-ul-Haq, the Chief of Army Staff, deposed the government, proclaimed Martial Law and suspended the operation of the Constitution. Bhutto was tried for conspiracy to murder, convicted, and executed on 4 April 1979 after the Supreme Court upheld the conviction. The fairness of that trial has been a lasting political dispute; the legal fact is the court’s judgment and the hanging.

Zia-ul-Haq, 1977–1988

The third Martial Law lasted, in its full form, until 1985. Parliamentary politics were suspended. Zia promised elections, postponed them, and in 1978 assumed the presidency (National Assembly list: 16 September 1978 – 17 August 1988). Islamisation became state policy: the Hudood Ordinances (1979), Zakat and Ushr, blasphemy-law expansions, and a Federal Shariat Court. The Soviet intervention in Afghanistan in 1979 aligned Pakistan with a US- and Saudi-backed mujahideen war; the domestic political effect was to strengthen the military presidency and to militarise parts of the Afghan-border society.

Non-party elections to the National Assembly were held on 25 February 1985 (ECP). Muhammad Khan Junejo became Prime Minister on 23 March 1985. The Constitution was revived with the Eighth Amendment, including Article 58(2)(b) — the presidential power to dissolve the National Assembly — and Article 2A. In May 1988 Zia dismissed the Junejo government under that power. On 17 August 1988 Zia died in a plane crash near Bahawalpur, together with the US ambassador and Pakistani senior officers. Ghulam Ishaq Khan, Chairman of the Senate, became President. Party-based general elections followed on 16 November 1988.

The political consequence of the Zia years was a constitution that looked parliamentary but could be interrupted from the presidency; a party system that returned, but with Islamist and Afghan-war aftershocks; and a judiciary that, in the Nusrat Bhutto case (1977), had granted the Martial Law regime a doctrine of necessity. Those three facts framed the 1990s.

Elected governments, 1988–1999

The eleven years after Zia are often called a democratic decade. They were elected, and they were repeatedly interrupted. Article 58(2)(b) was used to dismiss governments. Benazir Bhutto of the PPP became Prime Minister on 2 December 1988, the first woman to head a democratic government in a Muslim-majority country. President Ghulam Ishaq Khan dismissed her government on 6 August 1990. After a caretaker interval under Ghulam Mustafa Jatoi, Nawaz Sharif of the Islami Jamhoori Ittehad / PML became Prime Minister on 6 November 1990.

On 18 April 1993 Ishaq Khan dismissed Nawaz Sharif and appointed Balakh Sher Mazari as caretaker. The Supreme Court restored Sharif on 26 May 1993. The dual confrontation of President and Prime Minister ended in both men’s resignation in July 1993, a caretaker government under Moeenuddin (Moin) Qureshi, and elections on 6 October 1993. Benazir Bhutto returned as Prime Minister on 19 October 1993. President Farooq Leghari, her former party colleague, dismissed her government on 5 November 1996. After Malik Meraj Khalid’s caretaker cabinet, Nawaz Sharif won a large majority in the 3 February 1997 election and took office on 17 February 1997.

The Thirteenth Amendment (1997) removed Article 58(2)(b). The Fourteenth added an anti-defection clause. In May 1998, after India’s nuclear tests, Pakistan conducted its own tests in Balochistan. Relations between the Sharif government and the military command deteriorated after the 1999 Kargil conflict with India. On 12 October 1999 General Pervez Musharraf, Chief of Army Staff, took power, dismissed the government and declared himself Chief Executive. The National Assembly records Sharif’s second elected tenure as ending on 12 October 1999.

Musharraf era, 1999–2008

Musharraf’s first years were extra-constitutional: a Provisional Constitution Order, a military presidency in practice, and a 2000 Supreme Court judgment that granted another doctrine of necessity with a three-year election deadline. He assumed the presidency on 20 June 2001 (National Assembly list). After the 11 September 2001 attacks the government aligned with the United States in the Afghanistan war, a choice that reshaped domestic security politics for the next two decades.

A Legal Framework Order (2002) and general elections on 10 October 2002 produced a National Assembly in which the Pakistan Muslim League (Quaid-e-Azam) was the largest party. Zafarullah Jamali, then Chaudhry Shujaat Hussain, then Shaukat Aziz served as Prime Minister. The Seventeenth Amendment (2003) absorbed the LFO into the Constitution and restored a presidential dissolution power. A local-government “devolution” plan (2001) created a new tier of nazims, bypassing the provinces.

In March 2007 Musharraf suspended Chief Justice Iftikhar Muhammad Chaudhry. The resulting lawyers’ movement became the widest urban professional protest in decades. On 3 November 2007 Musharraf proclaimed emergency, suspended the Constitution and dismissed a large number of superior-court judges. Benazir Bhutto had returned from exile in October 2007 under a National Reconciliation Ordinance; she was assassinated in Rawalpindi on 27 December 2007. General elections, postponed to 18 February 2008, returned the PPP as the largest party. Musharraf resigned the presidency on 18 August 2008 facing impeachment. Asif Ali Zardari was elected President on 9 September 2008.

Parliamentary sequence, 2008–2018

The 2008 election produced a PPP-led coalition. Yousaf Raza Gillani became Prime Minister on 25 March 2008. The Eighteenth Amendment, passed in April 2010, is the central constitutional event of the period: it re-parliamentaryised the 1973 text, removed 58(2)(b), devolved the concurrent legislative list to the provinces, renamed the NWFP as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and strengthened the Election Commission. Fiscal federalism continued through the National Finance Commission. Gillani was convicted of contempt by the Supreme Court in 2012 and ceased to be Prime Minister; Raja Pervez Ashraf succeeded him on 22 June 2012. The Assembly completed its five-year term.

The 11 May 2013 general election returned the Pakistan Muslim League-Nawaz as the largest party. That transfer — one elected government to another — was the first in the post-2008 parliamentary sequence. Nawaz Sharif became Prime Minister on 5 June 2013. In 2016 the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists published the Panama Papers, which included companies linked to Sharif’s family. The Supreme Court, in its Panama case judgments of 2017, disqualified Nawaz Sharif from office; he left on 28 July 2017. Shahid Khaqan Abbasi served as Prime Minister until the Assembly’s term ended on 31 May 2018. The disqualification was a court judgment. Political parties have offered competing interpretations of its motives; this archive records the judgment and the change of office, not those interpretations as fact.

2018–2022: PTI government and no-confidence

General elections were held on 25 July 2018. The Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf emerged as the largest party and formed a coalition government. Imran Khan took oath as Prime Minister on 18 August 2018 (National Assembly list: 18 August 2018 – 10 April 2022). Arif Alvi was elected President. The COVID-19 pandemic, an IMF-supported adjustment, and a foreign-policy opening toward Russia in early 2022 were the main governing contexts. Opposition parties, grouped as the Pakistan Democratic Movement, alleged that the 2018 election had been managed; the PTI government rejected that claim. Neither allegation nor denial is treated here as a finding.

On 10 April 2022 the National Assembly passed a vote of no confidence against Imran Khan, the first successful use of that constitutional instrument against a Prime Minister. Shehbaz Sharif was elected Prime Minister the next day (National Assembly: 11 April 2022 – 14 August 2023). Khan and the PTI described the vote as the product of a foreign conspiracy and a civil-military alignment; the incoming coalition described it as a parliamentary majority exercising a constitutional right. The recorded fact is the vote and the change of office.

2022–2024 transition

Shehbaz Sharif’s first prime ministership ran until the National Assembly’s term ended. He left office on 14 August 2023. Anwaar-ul-Haq Kakar headed a caretaker government from 14 August 2023 to 3 March 2024 (National Assembly dates). General elections, delayed beyond the usual ninety-day window, were held on 8 February 2024. On 1 March 2024 the National Assembly elected Sardar Ayaz Sadiq as Speaker and Syed Ghulam Mustafa Shah as Deputy Speaker. On 3 March 2024 the Assembly elected Shehbaz Sharif Prime Minister again. On 9 March 2024 the presidential electoral college elected Asif Ali Zardari; he took office on 10 March 2024. Yusuf Raza Gilani became Chairman of the Senate on 9 April 2024.

The 8 February 2024 National Assembly election did not give any party a majority of general seats. Independent candidates, a large number of them publicly associated with PTI after the party was not allowed to use its election symbol, won the largest block of general seats (ECP-announced results placed independents at 101 of the seats then declared). PML-N won 75 general seats and PPP 54. A coalition led by PML-N, with PPP and the Muttahida Qaumi Movement-Pakistan among its partners, formed the federal government. Opposition parties alleged interference and result manipulation; the ECP published constituency results and rejected the charge as a finding. Subsequent litigation over reserved seats for women and non-Muslims changed the Assembly’s party arithmetic; by 2025 the National Assembly secretariat recorded PML-N as the largest parliamentary party after reserved-seat notifications.

Two constitutional packages defined the mid-term of this Assembly. The Twenty-sixth Amendment (assented 21 October 2024) changed the method of appointing the Chief Justice and the composition of the Judicial Commission. The Twenty-seventh Amendment (assented 13 November 2025) created a Federal Constitutional Court and rewrote Article 243 to establish a Chief of Defence Forces, a post held concurrently by the Chief of Army Staff, with the Chairman Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee abolished from 27 November 2025. In May 2025, after a military conflict with India, President Zardari and Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif conferred the rank of Field Marshal on Chief of Army Staff Syed Asim Munir. Parliament later passed related defence legislation, including in 2026.

Parliamentary life in 2026 included passage of the federal budget for fiscal year 2026–27 in June, after an opposition walkout, and government–opposition correspondence on the appointment of a new Chief Election Commissioner. The five-year term of CEC Sikandar Sultan Raja expired on 26 January 2025; as of September 2026 consultations under Article 213 were still a live parliamentary issue. Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar remained a principal figure of the coalition. Provincial governments elected in 2024 continued: Maryam Nawaz Sharif as Chief Minister of Punjab from 26 February 2024 — the first woman to hold that office — with PPP-led government in Sindh and coalition administrations in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan subject to the 2024 provincial results and later floor changes.

The 2024 general election

Polling for the National Assembly and the four provincial assemblies was held on 8 February 2024. The National Assembly’s general seats are filled by first-past-the-post contests; reserved seats for women and non-Muslims are then allocated to parties by formula. PTI was not permitted to use its election symbol; candidates associated with the party ran as independents, many of whom later joined the Sunni Ittehad Council.

ECP-announced results for general seats, as reported when the Commission completed declarations, placed independent candidates first (101), PML-N second (75) and PPP third (54), with MQM-P on 17 and smaller parties on the remainder of the then-declared seats. Shehbaz Sharif was elected Prime Minister on 3 March 2024 with 201 votes in the National Assembly, against 92 for Omar Ayub Khan, the candidate of the SIC / PTI-backed opposition. Asif Ali Zardari was elected President on 9 March 2024.

The election is politically sensitive. Opposition parties alleged interference, delay in result transmission and alteration of counts. The ECP published Form-47/Form-49 constituency results and did not accept those allegations as proven. Domestic and international observer groups recorded both administrative failures and a competitive race. Reserved-seat litigation continued into 2024–25 and changed the Assembly’s notified party position. This archive therefore states the ECP general-seat snapshot, the parliamentary election of the Prime Minister and President, and the existence of a dispute — not a finding on who “really won.”

Parties, people, representation

Political Parties & Leaders

Pakistani parties have been national, regional, religious, ethnic and personal-machine organisations, often several of those at once. Founding dates are sometimes disputed because parties split, renamed themselves, or revived older labels. The notes below record historical role, not a ranking of virtue or strength.

1906 / 1947Pakistan Muslim League and its factions

The All-India Muslim League (1906) was the independence vehicle. After 1947 the Pakistan Muslim League fragmented repeatedly. Convention League under Ayub, Council League, PML-N, PML-Q and smaller groups all claim League ancestry. The League’s historical significance is the founding of the state; its later significance is that “Muslim League” became a reusable label rather than a single organisation.

1967Pakistan Peoples Party

Founded 30 November 1967 in Lahore by Zulfikar Ali Bhutto. Combined a populist, left-leaning programme with a strong Sindh base after the 1970s. Governed federally in 1971–77, 1988–90, 1993–96, 2008–13, and as a coalition partner in 2022– and 2024–. Benazir Bhutto and later Asif Ali Zardari and Bilawal Bhutto Zardari have been its principal national figures.

1993 labelPakistan Muslim League-Nawaz

The Nawaz Sharif faction of the League, consolidated after the IJI period (1988–93). Governed federally in 1990–93, 1997–99, 2013–18, and from 2022 in coalition (Shehbaz Sharif). Strongest historically in Punjab. Maryam Nawaz became Chief Minister of Punjab in 2024.

2002Pakistan Muslim League-Q

Formed around supporters of the Musharraf-era parliamentary experiment. Largest party after the 2002 election. Later a smaller coalition partner. Distinct from PML-N despite the shared League name.

1996Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf

Founded 25 April 1996 by Imran Khan. Became a national governing party after 2018. After 2022 it was the principal opposition force; in 2024 its candidates largely contested as independents after the party symbol was withheld. Competing accounts of 2018 and 2024 elections are recorded in the election archive, not resolved here.

1949 (East Pakistan)Awami League

Founded in East Pakistan; under Sheikh Mujibur Rahman it won the 1970 majority on the Six Points. After 1971 it became the founding party of Bangladesh and ceased to be a Pakistani party. It belongs in this archive because the 1970–71 crisis is Pakistani political history.

1984Muttahida Qaumi Movement

Grew from the All-Pakistan Muhajir Students Organisation (1978) into the Mohajir / Muttahida Qaumi Movement in 1984 under Altaf Hussain. Dominant in urban Sindh, especially Karachi, in several elections. Later split; MQM-Pakistan has been a federal coalition partner, including after 2024.

1986Awami National Party

Successor, via the National Awami Party, to the Khudai Khidmatgar / Wali Khan tradition of Pashtun nationalist electoral politics. Governed Khyber Pakhtunkhwa in coalition periods; Abdul Wali Khan and later Asfandyar Wali Khan are its principal dynastic leaders.

1941Jamaat-e-Islami

Founded by Abul A’la Maududi on 26 August 1941. Cadre-based Islamist party; influential in constitution-making debates, the 1977 PNA, the Afghan-war years, and as a coalition or opposition force. Electoral seat totals have usually been modest relative to its organisational weight.

1945 / later splitsJamiat Ulema-e-Islam

Deobandi ulama party, later split; the Fazlur Rehman faction (JUI-F) has been the most consequential in national coalitions, including the 2002 MMA and later assemblies. Distinct from Jamaat-e-Islami.

2002 allianceMuttahida Majlis-e-Amal

Electoral alliance of religious parties, strongest in the 2002 election in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan. Later revived in varying form. An alliance, not a single party.

RegionalBalochistan and Pashtun parties

Balochistan National Party (Mengal and other factions), National Party, Balochistan Awami Party, and Pakhtunkhwa Milli Awami Party (Mehmood Khan Achakzai) have been the main electoral vehicles of Baloch and southern-Pashtun regional politics. PkMAP’s Achakzai was Leader of the Opposition in the National Assembly in 2026.

The Pakistan National Alliance (1977) was a multi-party opposition movement, not a standing party. Smaller groups — Tehreek-e-Labbaik Pakistan, Istehkam-e-Pakistan Party, National Peoples Party, and others — appear in specific elections and coalitions. They are omitted as cards where their national-historical weight is limited.

Federation and the provinces

Provinces & Federalism

Pakistan is a federation whose internal map has been rewritten several times. The original western units were the Punjab, Sindh, the North-West Frontier Province and Balochistan (then a mix of province, states and tribal areas), plus princely states and the tribal agencies. East Bengal was the majority-population wing. Islamabad Capital Territory, Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Jammu and Kashmir sit in different constitutional categories: ICT is a federal territory represented in parliament; GB and AJK have their own legislatures and are not provinces of Pakistan under the 1973 Constitution.

One Unit and its undoing

On 14 October 1955 the western provinces and states were merged into a single “West Pakistan” to create a two-unit federation with East Pakistan. The stated purpose was administrative simplification and parity. The political effect in the smaller western provinces was the loss of their own ministries and the sense that Punjab dominated the new unit. Yahya Khan dissolved One Unit with effect from 1 July 1970, restoring Punjab, Sindh, NWFP and Balochistan in time for the 1970 election. That restoration is the basis of the present four-province map.

The 1973 federation and the Eighteenth Amendment

The 1973 Constitution listed federal subjects and, until 2010, a concurrent list. Provinces had assemblies, chief ministers and governors. In practice the centre remained strong, especially under military-led governments. The Eighteenth Amendment (2010) abolished the concurrent list, transferred a large body of legislative and administrative work to the provinces (including school and health administration), renamed the NWFP as Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and restated the Council of Common Interests as the federal–provincial coordinating forum. National Finance Commission awards govern the vertical and horizontal share of taxes. Federalism in Pakistan is therefore not only a map: it is a fiscal argument that reappears at every NFC and every budget.

Provincial political traditions

Punjab has been the demographic and electoral centre of gravity of the post-1971 state. PML-N, PPP (in earlier decades), PTI (especially 2018 and 2024) and, historically, the Unionist then League traditions have competed here. Lahore, Rawalpindi–Islamabad’s military-bureaucratic complex, and the central Punjab cities have supplied a disproportionate share of federal office-holders. Maryam Nawaz’s 2024 chief ministership is a new fact in a province that had not previously had a woman in that office.

Sindh has a dual political geography: rural Sindh, where the PPP has been the principal electoral force since the 1970s, and urban Sindh, especially Karachi and Hyderabad, where MQM (and later its successors and rivals, including PTI and religious parties) have been decisive. The 1980s ethnic violence in Karachi is part of the province’s political history, as is the long argument over rural–urban resource allocation.

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (NWFP until 2010) has alternated among ANP, religious alliances (notably MMA in 2002), PML-N, and PTI, which won the provincial assembly in 2018 and remained the principal force in 2024. The 25th Amendment’s merger of FATA into the province (2018) extended the provincial franchise and courts to the former tribal districts — a constitutional change whose administrative completion is still a political subject.

Balochistan is the largest province by area and the smallest by population. Its politics combine sardari and party forms, repeated federal interventions, nationalist parties (BNP and others), and, at times, insurgency. Neither “tribal” nor “nationalist” is an adequate single label. Quetta’s mixed population, the Mekran coast, and the gas- and mineral-bearing districts have different political economies. Federal–provincial conflict over resources is a constitutional as well as a security issue.

Islamabad is the federal capital, represented in the National Assembly but without a provincial assembly. Its local government has been repeatedly redesigned. Politically it is a national stage rather than a provincial system.

Local government

Local government has been used both to widen participation and to bypass parties. Ayub Khan’s Basic Democracies (1959) created an electoral college for the presidency. Zia-ul-Haq’s 1979 local-government system was non-party in design. Musharraf’s 2001 Devolution Plan created district nazims and, again, a local tier stronger than the provinces for a time. After 2010, local government became a provincial subject; each province has written its own local government laws, with uneven elections and frequent suspension. The historical pattern is that military-led governments have often invested in local government as a substitute for provincial and parliamentary politics, while elected provincial governments have often been reluctant to devolve in turn.

Crises and the courts

Major Political Crises

The cards below are a documentary index, not a league table of blame. Where events are disputed, the card says who alleged, who ruled, or who reported.

1948–51 succession shock

Jinnah’s death (1948) and Liaquat’s assassination (1951) removed the two founding office-holders and shifted power toward the bureaucratic Governor-General.

1954 Assembly dissolution

Governor-General Ghulam Muhammad dissolved the first Constituent Assembly. Maulvi Tamizuddin Khan’s challenge failed in the Federal Court (Justice Munir), which privileged the Governor-General’s powers.

1958 Martial Law

Iskander Mirza abrogated the Constitution on 7 October; Ayub Khan took the presidency on 27 October. Dosso (1958) treated the coup as a new legal order.

1968–69 anti-Ayub movement

Urban protest, student and labour mobilisation, and elite defection forced Ayub’s resignation and a second Martial Law under Yahya Khan.

1970–71 constitutional crisis

Awami League majority not transferred into government; military operation in East Pakistan; India–Pakistan war; Bangladesh independence. See the 1970 and East Pakistan sections.

1977 election and PNA

Official results gave PPP a large majority. The Pakistan National Alliance alleged rigging. Street movement and failed talks preceded the 5 July coup.

1988–99 dismissals

Article 58(2)(b) was used to dismiss elected governments in 1988/90, 1993 and 1996. The Supreme Court restored Nawaz Sharif in May 1993, then both President and Prime Minister resigned.

1999 coup

On 12 October 1999 the army command dismissed the Sharif government. Musharraf became Chief Executive, later President. A Supreme Court judgment in 2000 granted a time-limited doctrine of necessity.

2007 judicial crisis and emergency

Suspension of the Chief Justice (March) produced the lawyers’ movement. Emergency of 3 November 2007 suspended the Constitution and removed judges. Musharraf resigned in 2008.

Benazir Bhutto assassination

27 December 2007, Rawalpindi. A UN commission later examined the killing. Criminal and political accountability remain contested; the fact of the assassination is not.

2014 protests

PTI and allied sit-ins in Islamabad contested the 2013 election result. The government was not displaced. Competing claims of rigging were not converted into a court-ordered re-election.

2017 Panama disqualification

The Supreme Court disqualified Nawaz Sharif from office. That is a court judgment. Parties disagree about its political meaning.

2018 election controversies

PTI formed a government as largest party. Opposition parties alleged pre-poll management. No election tribunal rewrote the national result.

2022 no-confidence

10 April 2022: first successful parliamentary vote of no confidence against a Prime Minister. PTI alleged a foreign-backed conspiracy; the coalition called it a constitutional majority. The recorded fact is the vote.

2023–24 election delay and 8 February 2024

Elections were held after the usual ninety-day window. Opposition parties alleged result interference. The ECP notified winners. Reserved-seat cases then changed parliamentary arithmetic.

2024–25 constitutional packages

Twenty-sixth Amendment (October 2024) and Twenty-seventh Amendment (November 2025) remade judicial appointment and military command clauses. Opposition parties described both as rushed; the government described both as reform. Both are now part of the official constitutional text.

Judiciary and politics — a historical note

This is not legal advice. It is a record of constitutional cases that changed political possibility. The Tamizuddin case (1955) and Dosso (1958) enlarged extra-parliamentary power. Asma Jilani (1972) rejected Dosso and held Yahya’s Martial Law illegal — after it had already ended. Nusrat Bhutto (1977) granted Zia a doctrine of necessity. The 1993 restoration of Nawaz Sharif was a rare judicial check on 58(2)(b). The 2000 Musharraf validation and the 2007 emergency, followed by the restoration of judges in 2009, book-ended the lawyers’ movement. The Panama judgments (2017) removed a sitting Prime Minister. After 2022 a further series of cases involving PTI, election symbols, reserved seats and bail became part of ordinary political competition. In each instance this archive distinguishes the court’s order from the political interpretation placed on it.

How the system is supposed to work

Political institutions

Offices below are described as they stand in the 1973 Constitution after the Eighteenth Amendment, with notes on how they have differed in other periods.

Head of statePresident

Elected by an electoral college of Parliament and the provincial assemblies for five years. After 2010 the President acts on the advice of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, except where the Constitution says otherwise. Under 1962, and under 58(2)(b), the office was executive in a stronger sense.

Head of governmentPrime Minister

Elected by the National Assembly; commands the federal Cabinet. The working centre of a parliamentary government — except in periods when a military president, a dissolving civilian president, or a Chief Martial Law Administrator held the effective executive.

Lower houseNational Assembly

Population-basis house. General seats plus reserved seats for women and non-Muslims. Five-year term unless dissolved. Originates money bills. Elects the Prime Minister and the Speaker.

Upper houseSenate

Created by the 1973 Constitution. Provincial (and territorial) representation on a more equal basis than the Assembly. Chairman is first in line to act as President. Cannot be dissolved with the Assembly.

Apex courtsSupreme Court / FCC

Until 2025 the Supreme Court was the apex constitutional court. The Twenty-seventh Amendment created a Federal Constitutional Court with constitutional jurisdiction. Historical political cases are listed in the crises section. This page does not give legal advice.

ElectionsElection Commission

A constitutional body. Conducts federal and provincial elections. The Twentieth Amendment set out appointment procedures involving government and opposition. CEC tenure and appointments were a live parliamentary issue in 2025–26.

FederalismCouncil of Common Interests

Federal–provincial forum chaired by the Prime Minister. Strengthened in the 1973 design and again by the Eighteenth Amendment. Complements the National Finance Commission on fiscal federalism.

Islamic provisionsCouncil of Islamic Ideology

Advisory constitutional body on whether laws are consistent with Islamic injunctions. Influential as a recommending institution, not as a legislature.

ProvincesAssemblies and governments

Each province has an elected assembly, a chief minister and a governor. Governors are federal appointees acting on advice in the parliamentary periods. Provincial autonomy expanded sharply in 2010.

TransitionsCaretaker governments

Neutral cabinets appointed to hold elections, now under a constitutional procedure (Twentieth Amendment and later practice). They are not a parliamentary government and are not a Martial Law administration — a distinction this archive keeps.
