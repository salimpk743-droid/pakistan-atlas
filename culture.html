Living culture

One country, many mother tongues. Culture here means daily life, not a museum label.

Pakistan’s official language is Urdu, and English is used in law and higher education. At home people speak Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi, Balochi, Saraiki, Hindko, Brahui, Shina, Balti, Burushaski, Wakhi, Kashmiri and many others. A good atlas names the language of a valley without ranking it.

Dress and cloth

Shalwar kameez is common across the country, with regional cuts: the Sindhi ajrak and topi, the embroidered Balochi doch, the Phulkari of Punjab, and the embroidered caps of Hunza and Chitral. Truck art — bright poetry on lorries — is a moving gallery on every highway.

Music and poetry

Qawwali from the Sufi shrines, the rubab of Peshawar, Sufi kalam in Saraiki and Sindhi, and modern film and pop from Lahore and Karachi all belong on the same map. Allama Iqbal’s Persian and Urdu verse, and the folk epics of Heer and Sassui Punhun, are still taught because they explain how people once imagined justice and love.

Food as geography

Sajji on the Baloch coast and highlands, Peshawari chapli kebab, Lahori breakfasts, Sindhi biryani and fish on the Indus, Hunza apricot oil and buckwheat — food follows climate and trade routes. Write recipes with the district they come from, not as a national stereotype.

Faith and gathering

Most citizens are Muslim. Hindu, Christian, Sikh and Kalash communities are part of the same civic story and should be described with the same care. Urs gatherings, Eid, Christmas in Gojra or Karachi, and Vaisakhi in Punjab towns are public facts, not arguments.
