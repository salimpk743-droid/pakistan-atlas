How to read this archive. Pakistani culture is plural. Urban Karachi is not rural Sindh; Lahore is not southern Punjab; Peshawar is not Chitral; Quetta is not Makran; Gilgit-Baltistan and Azad Jammu & Kashmir hold several local traditions, not one mountain costume. Shared national habits — Urdu as a link language, shalwar kameez as everyday dress in many settings, Eid as a widely observed festival among Muslims — sit beside regional, linguistic, religious and class differences. Poetry and language are treated more fully in the Literature & Poetry Archive; landforms that shape culture in the Geography Archive; games and sport history in the Sports Archive; civic change in the Political History Archive.

Culture at a glance

A country of overlapping cultures

Pakistan is home to many regional cultures, languages, landscapes and traditions. Punjabi folk heritage, Sindhi shrine and textile worlds, Pashtun hospitality codes, Baloch and Brahui practices, the mountain communities of Gilgit-Baltistan, the valley cultures of Azad Jammu & Kashmir, and the migrant mix of Islamabad and Karachi do not collapse into one “national costume.” What is shared is a long conversation: languages that borrow from one another, foods that travel with migration, music that moves from shrine to radio to phone, and a hospitality ethic that many communities name in their own words.

The dimensions of this archive — language, food, clothing, music, poetry, crafts, festivals, architecture, oral tradition, family and community life, hospitality, religious practice, regional identity and modern urban culture — overlap. Music can be liturgical, folk, classical or commercial. Food can be strongly regional and still eaten far from its origin. Languages cross provincial lines: Hindko is important in parts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and is also heard in northern Punjab; Saraiki is a major mother tongue of southern Punjab and is not “just dialect” for the people who speak it.

Culture changes. A 1970s village wedding, a 1990s PTV serial, and a 2020s campus café are all Pakistani social facts. This page is a cultural atlas, not a frozen museum case. For provincial pages open provinces and the district list.

Cultures of Pakistan

Regional cultural atlas

Each unit below has its own grain. The colour bars are a page design, not ethnic flags.

Punjab

The most populous province, with Punjabi and a large Saraiki-speaking south, plus Urdu in cities. Folk poetry of the qissa tradition — Heer-Ranjha as told by Waris Shah, and the wider cycle of Sassi, Sohni and Mirza-Sahiban — is literary culture, not a claim that every village stages the same play. Sufi shrines (from Data Darbar in Lahore to the saints of Multan and Pakpattan) organise music, langar and annual urs. Bhangra and giddha are harvest and celebratory dances associated with parts of Punjab, now also staged and filmed; they are not a daily habit of every Punjabi household. Dhol, algoza and folk singing sit beside qawwali and ghazal. Phulkari embroidery, pottery towns, and the painted poetry of truck bodies are craft geographies. Makki di roti and saag, lassi, and tandoor breads are widely cited rural and town foods; nihari and rich gravies belong as much to urban Muslim kitchens as to any “village menu.” Lahore’s walled city, film and college culture is not the same as a canal-colony town or Cholistan’s pastoral edge. Wedding sequences (mehndi, barat, walima) vary by family, sect and class. Melas such as Mela Chiraghan in Lahore remain shrine-fair hybrids. Do not treat “Punjabi culture” as a single rural postcard.

Sindh

Sindhi language, Ajrak block-printed cloth, and the embroidered Sindhi topi are among the most widely recognised regional markers — and they are still not the whole map. Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai’s Shah jo Risalo is the literary centre of gravity; shrine music, dhamal and the urs calendar (Sehwan, Bhit Shah and others) organise large public gatherings. Ralli quilts, Hala crafts, and pottery sit in artisan towns. Sai bhaji, Sindhi biryani, and coastal fish dishes are regional kitchens, not a single provincial thali. Tharparkar has Hindu and Muslim folk worlds, drought pastoralism and desert dress that differ from the irrigated rice-and-cotton belt. Thatta and Makli are funerary and historic landscapes. Hyderabad is an older Sindhi urban node. Karachi is a migrant metropolis — Urdu-speaking, Pashtun, Punjabi, Baloch, Sindhi, Memon, Gujarati, Goan, Christian, Hindu and others — and cannot be labelled simply “Sindhi culture.” Coastal and delta communities have boat, mangrove and fishery traditions of their own.

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa

Pashto is the majority mother tongue in PBS 2023 tables for the province, but KP is not only Pashtun. Pashtunwali — a named ethical vocabulary of honour, hospitality (melmastia), refuge and reputation — is discussed in anthropology as a living, debated code, not a statute. The hujra as a men’s guest-and-council space, and the jirga as a dispute-settling assembly, are social institutions with history; they are not the government of every neighbourhood, and they have been both valued and criticised. Rahman Baba and Khushal Khan Khattak stand in Pashto poetry. Rubab, mangey (pot drum) and attan belong to particular dance and music settings; Khattak dance is a martial-display form associated with some Pashtun groups, not a provincial anthem. Chapli kebab and naan-based meals are widely associated with Peshawar and adjoining districts. Chitral (Khowar-speaking), the Kalash valleys, Swat, Hazara (Hindko-speaking towns such as Abbottabad and Mansehra), Kohistan, and the merged former-FATA districts each have their own speech, dress and music. Reducing KP to one turban is a map error.

Balochistan

Balochi and Brahui are both major languages of the province; Pashto is also a large mother tongue in the north, around Zhob and the Quetta hinterland. Baloch embroidery, long dresses and needlework, dambura and suroz in some musical settings, and oral dastan traditions are part of named Baloch cultural practice — among some communities, not as a uniform tribal costume. Pastoral and camel-linked livelihoods remain important in dry tracts; they are not the only economy. Coastal Makran and Gwadar have fishery, boat and later-port cultures distinct from highland Kalat. Sajji and landhi (dried meat) are widely cited Baloch foods; Makran also eats fish. Quetta is a multilingual highland city (Baloch, Pashtun, Hazara, Punjabi, Urdu-speaking and others). Sibi is known for a historic seasonal gathering (Sibi Mela). Do not flatten the largest province by area into one desert stereotype.

Gilgit-Baltistan

GB is several language worlds: Shina, Balti, Burushaski, Wakhi, Khowar on the Chitral-Ghizer edge, and others. Valley architecture — stone, timber, and later concrete — responds to altitude and snow. Polo, including the high-ground gathering at Shandur (shared in fame with Chitral), is sport-as-festival. Apricots, walnut, and buckwheat or wheat breads, with dishes such as chapshoro and mamtu in some valleys, are mountain kitchens, not a single GB menu. Wool, embroidery and woodwork are craft skills with local names. Hunza, Gilgit, Skardu, Ghizer, Astore and Baltistan do not share one dress or one faith history: Ismaili, Sunni and Shia communities are all part of the map. Treat GB as a cluster of valley cultures under extreme relief — the geography archive explains why settlement is linear.

Azad Jammu & Kashmir

AJK’s speech map includes Pahari-Pothwari varieties, Hindko in some tracts, Gojri among Gujjar communities, and Kashmiri in parts of the population; exact shares are not treated here as a fake census of “the Kashmiri language” for the whole territory. Shawl and embroidery traditions, woodwork, and mountain-valley building are widely associated with Kashmiri craft worlds — including practices that also exist across the Line of Control; this archive records culture, not a political claim. Folk song, wedding custom and rice-and-meat dishes vary by valley. Muzaffarabad, Mirpur (with a large British-Pakistani diaspora link), Poonch and Neelum are different social geographies. After 2005, reconstruction itself became part of the built culture of the capital.

Islamabad Capital Territory

Islamabad does not have a single traditional ethnic culture. It is a planned federal capital on Potohar land, filled by migration from every province, a diplomatic community, universities, and the surrounding Punjabi-Potohar countryside. Lok Virsa’s Heritage Museum and the Pakistan Monument sit on Shakarparian as national display spaces. Faisal Mosque is a modern landmark, not a “folk” building. Cafés, bookshops, art galleries and campus societies are the everyday culture of sectors; Saidpur and the Margalla foothills keep older village textures at the edge. PBS 2023 language tables for ICT show a mix in which Punjabi, Urdu and Pashto are all substantial — a census fact, not a cultural ranking.

Speech

Languages of Pakistan

Urdu is the national language and a major urban link; English is used in law, higher education and parts of the state. Neither is the mother tongue of most households. PBS 2023 mother-tongue tables (the language questions cover the enumerated population used in those tables, not a ranking of cultural worth) show Punjabi as the largest single mother tongue (about 37%), then Pashto (about 18%), Sindhi (about 14%), Saraiki (about 12%) and Urdu (about 9%). The same tables name Balochi, Hindko, Brahui, Mewati, Kohistani, Kashmiri, Shina, Balti and Kalasha among others. Secondary reports of exact counts differ slightly with the denominator they choose; this page therefore uses shares, not a fake league table of “importance.”

Languages not always visible in a short national table still organise whole valleys: Burushaski in Hunza-Nagar, Wakhi on the high northern approaches, Khowar in Chitral, and others. Scripts include Perso-Arabic (Nastaliq for Urdu and many others), and distinct practices for Sindhi orthography; Roman and English sit in advertising and phones. Multilingualism is normal: a Karachi shop may use Urdu, a Punjab village Punjabi, a Peshawar hujra Pashto, and English in a university seminar — sometimes in the same family.

Language is identity, literature, oral tradition and administration. It is also political. This archive does not settle debates about whether Saraiki or Hindko “count” as languages; it records that millions of people name them as mother tongues in the census. For writers and poetry see the Literature archive.

Kitchen geography

Food & culinary culture

Food follows climate, irrigation, faith (halal practice among Muslims; vegetarian and other kitchens among Hindu and some other households), class and migration. Dishes travel. Attribution below is “particularly associated with,” not “owned by.” This is not a recipe book.

Punjab

Saag with makki di roti in cooler months; lassi; tandoor breads; richer urban gravies including nihari in city Muslim kitchens; sweets that track weddings and urs. Southern Punjab and Saraiki-speaking districts have their own bread and meat habits. Lahore’s breakfast and street food is urban, not village.

Sindh

Sai bhaji; Sindhi biryani in many family versions; fish and rice on the Indus and coast; breads and sweets of irrigated towns. Thar cooking is not Hyderabad cooking. Karachi’s food map is migrant: bun kebab, biryani houses, Bohra and Memon kitchens, Pashtun tikka, and much else.

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa

Chapli kebab associated especially with Peshawar; naan and meat-heavy meals in many Pashtun settings; rice dishes influenced by Afghan and Central Asian kitchens in some households. Chitral and mountain districts eat differently from DI Khan. Hospitality meals are social events, not restaurant menus.

Balochistan

Sajji (slow-roasted meat) is widely cited; landhi as dried meat for lean seasons; breads of dry country; Makran seafood. Quetta’s city food is mixed. Do not reduce the province to one roast.

Gilgit-Baltistan

Chapshoro and mamtu in some valleys; apricot products; butter tea in some high communities; wheat and buckwheat breads. Altitude and winter storage shape the pantry more than any “GB restaurant list.”

AJK

Rice and meat dishes, mountain breads, and Kashmiri-influenced gravies in some families; Pahari home cooking is not identical to a Srinagar restaurant menu, and this page does not copy one.

Tea, seasonal fruit, and shared wedding rice dishes move across provinces with people. Geography — plains, coast, desert, mountain — still explains more than a flag.

Cloth

Dress, textiles & embroidery

Shalwar kameez is the most widely worn everyday outfit across genders and regions, with cuts, lengths and fabrics that change by climate, community, occasion and generation. The dupatta and various shawls add coverage, warmth and display. This is not “the national dress” in a legal sense; it is a practical majority habit that sits beside jeans, suits, school uniforms and ceremonial clothes.

Regional markers include Sindhi Ajrak and topi; Balochi heavy embroidery on women’s dress in some communities; Punjabi phulkari and related embroidery; Chitrali woollens and the pakol-style cap in northern mountain districts; Kalash women’s black robes, beadwork and headdresses in the valleys where Kalash religion is practised; Hunza and Baltistan caps and woollens; Kashmiri shawl traditions. Jewellery, especially for weddings, varies by community. Urban fashion weeks and bridal magazines remix these sources; that remix is contemporary culture, not “authentic village dress.” Clothing also marks religion, mourning, and work (the labourer’s shalwar is not the television hero’s).

Sound

Music of Pakistan

Music here is classical, folk, devotional and commercial — often in the same singer’s career. Hindustani classical training (khayal, thumri, instrumental gharanas) survived Partition in Pakistani cities, especially Lahore. Ghazal singing took Urdu poetry onto radio and mehfil. Folk streams include Punjabi and Saraiki song, Sindhi kafi and waee, Pashto tappa and ghazal, Balochi and Brahui song, and mountain repertoires that are barely present on national television.

Instruments

Rubab is central to Pashtun and some northern music; a 2024 UNESCO Representative List inscription for “art of crafting and playing rubab/rabab” names Afghanistan, Iran, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan — not Pakistan — so this page treats Pakistani rubab as a living regional instrument, not as that UNESCO file. Dhol and dholak carry Punjabi and shrine percussion. Algoza (paired flutes) is associated with some Punjabi and Sindhi folk. Harmonium and tabla became the standard accompaniment of ghazal and qawwali in the recording era. Sarangi, sitar and other classical strings remain specialist. Dambura and suroz appear in Baloch musical settings. Yaktaro/ektara and related drones appear in Sufi folk. Boreendo (also spelled bhorindo), a clay vessel flute of Sindh, was inscribed in 2025 on UNESCO’s List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding as a dying instrument and its skills.

Sufi music & qawwali

Qawwali is a South Asian Muslim devotional performance form, historically associated with Chishti and related shrine settings: a lead singer, chorus, harmonium, tabla or dholak, and a repertoire of hamd, naat, manqabat and ghazal. It is a tradition first, a recording industry second. Public names such as the Sabri Brothers, Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and Abida Parveen (whose work also includes kafi and other Sufi song) made shrine music global without turning every mehfil into a stadium. Dhamal — drum-led circling at some shrines, notably in Sindh — is a related but distinct bodily practice. Poetry (Bulleh Shah, Shah Latif, Khawaja Farid, Rahman Baba) is the literary fuel; see the Literature archive for poets, not lyrics copied here.

Movement

Dance & performance

Distinguish folk dance, ritual dance, ceremonial display, stage adaptation and pop choreography. None of the forms below is performed by “everyone in the province.”

Punjab

Bhangra (historically a men’s harvest dance, now also a staged and diasporic genre) and giddha (a women’s circle dance in many Punjabi settings). Luddi appears as a celebratory form in some districts. Stage bhangra is not the same as a village wedding.

Sindh

Ho Jamalo is a widely cited Sindhi celebratory song-dance. Dhamal at shrines is ritual/ecstatic, not a school concert item.

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa

Attan, in several local styles, is a circle dance associated with Pashtun gatherings. Khattak dance is a martial-display form linked to some Khattak and related groups. Kalash dances belong to Kalash seasonal festivals in the Chitral valleys. Chitrali performance is not attan by another name.

Balochistan & mountains

Regional Baloch dances exist in named community settings; this page does not invent a single “Baloch national dance.” GB and AJK have local circle and wedding dances that are poorly served by one label.

Hands

Crafts & artisan heritage

Craft is regional skill, not a souvenir category. Truck art — painted poetry, birds and landscapes on commercial vehicles — is a moving popular art of highways, not an “official national art.” Blue-glazed and related pottery is associated with Multan and some Sindh workshops. Hala (Sindh) is a known centre for tiles, wood and textiles. Ajrak block-printing and ralli quilting are Sindh (and adjoining) textile systems. Kashmiri shawl and woodwork, Balochi embroidery, Punjabi phulkari, Swat and Chitral wood carving, camel decoration in some pastoral settings, metalwork in old bazaars, and basketry in riverine tracts are documented artisan geographies. Lacquer work appears in named towns; this page does not assign every craft to a province by guesswork.

Urbanisation, cheap imports and changing taste have reduced some workshop lineages. Community and government programmes, including Lok Virsa festivals, try to keep skills visible. Commercialisation can both pay artisans and flatten designs into hotel décor.

Calendar

Festivals & celebrations

Religious, seasonal, harvest, shrine and civic calendars overlap. Not every celebration is nationwide. Status can change by law or security.

Muslim festivals. Eid-ul-Fitr and Eid-ul-Adha are widely observed among Muslims, with regional food and visiting customs. Muharram processions are central in Shia communities and also visible in mixed cities. Urs — death-anniversary gatherings at Sufi shrines — combine devotion, music, commerce and sometimes politics; they are local as much as “national.”

Other religious calendars. Christmas is a public fact in Christian neighbourhoods of Karachi, Punjab towns and elsewhere. Hindu communities in Sindh (and smaller groups elsewhere) observe Diwali, Holi and local temple festivals. Sikh gatherings occur at remaining gurdwaras and on historical commemorations. Kalash seasonal festivals — Joshi/Chilam Joshi in spring, Uchau/Uchal around harvest, and Chaumos/Choimus in winter — are the ritual year of Kalash valleys in Chitral, not a tourist product; outsider access to the most sacred parts has historically been limited.

Melas and civic events. Mela Chiraghan in Lahore, Sibi Mela in Balochistan, Lok Mela organised in Islamabad by Lok Virsa, and the Shandur polo gathering are named fairs. Independence Day (14 August) is a state civic festival. Nowruz is observed among some communities (including Hazara and others) and is part of a 2024 multinational UNESCO Representative List element that includes Pakistan; it is not a nationwide public holiday in the same way as Eid.

Basant. The spring kite festival associated especially with Lahore was restricted for many years because of injuries from chemical or metal string. Punjab passed a kite-flying law in 2025 and allowed a limited, regulated Basant in Lahore (including a 2026 window), with further regulated dates announced for 2027. This archive does not treat Basant as an unrestricted national tradition. Outside designated rules, kite flying has remained legally constrained in Punjab.

Social life

Family, weddings & hospitality

Extended-family networks, respect for elders, and hospitality (mehman-nawazi in Urdu; melmastia in Pashto) are widely reported social values. They are not practised identically, and they coexist with nuclear-family city flats, women’s paid work, and generation gaps.

Weddings

Among many Muslim families a sequence of engagement, mehndi, nikah, barat and walima is familiar — but order, scale, music and gender seating vary by sect, class, region and the couple. Hindu, Christian and Kalash marriages follow their own rites. A television “Shaadi” is not a field report. Dowry and lavish display are social debates, not timeless culture.

Hospitality & gathering spaces

The hujra in many Pashtun settings, the baithak or drawing room elsewhere, shrine courtyards, village dera, and urban drawing-rooms are different architectures of sitting together. Communal meals at weddings and urs, neighbourhood networks, and mosque or church committees are everyday institutions. Jirga and panchayat-like assemblies are historical dispute forums; they are not a substitute for courts, and their gender exclusions are part of the record.

Built worlds

Architecture & built heritage

Buildings on this soil predate the state of 1947. Indus cities such as Mohenjo-daro are Bronze Age urbanism, not “Pakistani culture” in the modern national sense. Gandharan Buddhist remains (Taxila, Takht-i-Bahi) belong to a classical Buddhist world. Islamic architecture arrives in waves; Mughal forts and gardens in Lahore are imperial. Sikh-era layers remain in Punjab’s historic cities. Colonial cantonments, railways and colleges reshaped towns. Vernacular houses — Sindh’s wind-catchers, Kalash timber, Hunza stone-and-wood, Makran coastal settlements, Punjabi courtyard houses — respond to climate. Modern Pakistani architecture includes the planned capital, Faisal Mosque, and ordinary concrete that now dominates most skylines.

Named places of study: Mohenjo-daro; Taxila; Takht-i-Bahi; Lahore Fort and Shalimar Gardens; Makli; Rohtas Fort; Multan shrines; Peshawar’s old city; Baltit Fort and Hunza valley architecture; Kalash houses. Quetta’s historic fabric was altered by earthquake and rebuilding. See UNESCO below for which of these are inscribed World Heritage properties.

UNESCO

World Heritage & living heritage

Pakistan ratified the World Heritage Convention on 23 July 1976. As of UNESCO’s State Party page, six properties are inscribed on the World Heritage List — all cultural. A larger tentative list (37 sites in UNESCO’s current count) is an inventory of possible future nominations, not inscription. Tentative-list places, including the Kalash valleys added in 2026, must not be called World Heritage Sites.

Intangible Cultural Heritage (UNESCO)

Pakistan ratified the 2003 ICH Convention on 7 October 2005. Elements on UNESCO’s Pakistan page:

  • Suri Jagek (2018) — Urgent Safeguarding List. Kalasha meteorological and astronomical practice of observing sun, moon and stars against local topography; used for sowing, herding and festival timing.
  • Falconry, a living human heritage (2021) — Representative List, multinational file that includes Pakistan.
  • Nawrouz / Nowruz (2024) — Representative List, multinational; Pakistan is among the states on the file.
  • Boreendo, Bhorindo (2025) — Urgent Safeguarding List. Sindh clay folk instrument, its melodies, knowledge and skills, described by UNESCO as ancient and dying.

Most living traditions on this page are not on those lists. Absence from UNESCO is not absence from culture.

Faith

Religious & spiritual traditions

A large majority of Pakistanis are Muslim. Sunni and Shia communities, Sufi shrine culture, and Ismaili communities in parts of Gilgit-Baltistan and elsewhere are all part of the Muslim map. This page does not do theology and does not treat one school as “the real Islam of Pakistan.”

Christian communities (urban and rural, including Punjab’s older Christian villages and Karachi parishes), Hindu communities especially in Sindh, Sikh communities around remaining gurdwaras, and the Kalash religious tradition of the Chitral valleys are civic and cultural facts. Places of worship — mosque, shrine, church, temple, gurdwara, Kalash sanctuary — are architecture and neighbourhood, not tourist décor. Pilgrimage and urs sit beside parish calendars and temple festivals. Minority communities are not “exotic attractions”; they are Pakistanis with languages, crafts and histories of their own.

Communities & cultural diversity

Kalash life includes language (Kalasha), dress, festivals and Suri Jagek knowledge; population in PBS language tables is small (thousands, not millions). Hindu Sindh includes temple towns, Thar pastoral culture and urban traders. Christian schools and churches have a long educational footprint. Sikh heritage is both a living minority and a layer of Punjab’s historic cities. Ismaili jamats in Hunza and adjoining valleys have distinctive communal institutions. Smaller linguistic groups (Wakhi, Burushaski speakers, Brahui, and others) are cultural communities even when they are not “religious minorities.” Token paragraphs help no one; the regional cards above are the fuller map.

Stories

Folklore & oral heritage

Folk tales, epic and romance cycles, Sufi anecdote, proverbs, riddles and professional or household storytelling are oral literature. The great Punjabi qissa romances — Heer-Ranjha, Sassi-Punnun, Sohni-Mahiwal, Mirza-Sahiban — and Sindh’s Umar-Marvi are literary and folk traditions, not verified chronicles of dated events. They explain how people have imagined love, justice, landscape and caste or clan honour. Dastan narration and village tale-cycles continue in some places; radio, cassette and now the phone have changed the audience without erasing the plots. Lok Virsa’s documentation mandate exists because oral forms are easy to lose.

Regional literatures — Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashto, Balochi, Brahui, Saraiki, Urdu — are cultural identity as well as books. They are archived at length on the Literature & Poetry page; this section only marks the join.

Image, word, screen

Visual arts, calligraphy, film & fashion

Visual arts

Miniature painting has Mughal and later South Asian roots on this soil; the National College of Arts in Lahore is a modern school of the form. Truck art is popular painting. Contemporary painting, sculpture and photography sit in PNCA’s National Art Gallery, private galleries in Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad, and university departments. Textile is both craft and fine art. This is not a celebrity catalogue.

Calligraphy & decorative arts

Islamic calligraphy, especially Nastaliq for Urdu and Persian, ornaments mosques, manuscripts and modern canvases. Sadequain is the most widely cited twentieth-century painter-calligrapher; many living artists continue the line. Mosque tile, wood mihrab and manuscript margins are decorative arts with regional workshops. Calligraphy is also a bridge to the Literature archive.

Film, television & popular culture

Lahore’s film industry (often nicknamed Lollywood) and Karachi’s later production, PTV drama from the 1970s onward, music television, comedy theatre, and now streaming platforms are how most people meet stories. Drama serials became a national conversation in Urdu. Cinema fortunes have risen and fallen; the point here is industry as culture, not box-office gossip. The National Academy of Performing Arts (NAPA) in Karachi, founded in the mid-2000s with Zia Mohyeddin as founding president, trains theatre and music students.

Fashion & contemporary dress

Bridal fashion, lawn seasons, fashion weeks in Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad, and designers who quote regional embroidery are a creative industry. Fusion is not “loss of culture”; it is how urban clothing actually works. This is not a shopping guide.

Places of culture

Cultural cities of Pakistan

Lahore

Mughal and later historic core, shrines, colleges, publishing, film memory, food streets, Basant debates.

Karachi

Port metropolis, migrant languages, theatre, galleries, NAPA, seafront popular culture. See Karachi South.

Multan

Shrine city, blue tile, mango and craft hinterland, southern Punjabi urbanity.

Peshawar

Old city, Qissa Khwani, rubab and Pashto literary life, gateway culture.

Quetta

Highland provincial capital, mixed languages, orchard hinterland, post-earthquake rebuilding.

Hyderabad & Thatta

Sindhi urban history; Makli’s funerary landscape; craft towns nearby.

Bahawalpur

Living now

Urban, rural and contemporary Pakistan

Modern urban Pakistan

Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad-Rawalpindi, Peshawar, Quetta, Multan, Faisalabad, Hyderabad and Gujranwala are not folk museums. Universities, cafés, shopping streets, bus terminals, television, fashion, contemporary music, and multilingual offices are the everyday. Migration fills cities with several mother tongues at once. Youth culture includes cricket fandom, drama clips, campus societies, and — documented, not predicted — heavy use of social networks and streaming. Street food and bazaars remain as important as malls.

Rural & village life

Rural Pakistan is the majority of the PBS 2023 population (about 61% rural in the four provinces plus ICT). Agriculture, livestock, weekly markets, crafts, oral tradition, village mosques and shrines, and wedding seasons structure time. That pattern is not the same in a canal-colony Punjabi village, a Sindh kacho settlement, a KP mountain hamlet, or a Baloch rangeland camp. Do not write “the village” as one place.

Culture & landscape

Mountains shape architecture, wool clothing, and transport calendars. Deserts shape mobility, pastoral crafts and well-centred settlement. Rivers shape dense farming, fish diets and flood memory. Coasts shape fishing, boats and seafood. Plains shape markets and dense kinship networks. The Geography archive is the physical half of the same sentence.

Sport as culture

Cricket is a mass spectator culture. Hockey has a deeper official-sport history. Polo in the high north, kabaddi and kushti (wrestling) in parts of the plains, tent pegging in some cavalry and festival settings, and children’s games such as gilli-danda are recreation with regional weight. The full sporting record sits in the Sports History Archive.

Pakistan today

Documented change includes urbanisation, satellite and then mobile media, Pakistani drama’s overseas audience, a fashion and lawn industry, diaspora return visits, and the digitisation of poetry and music. This page does not forecast the next decade. It records that culture was never frozen in 1947 and is not frozen now.

Continuity

A short cultural timeline

Modern Pakistani culture did not begin in 1947. The state is new; many languages, crafts and shrines are not.

Indus Valley

Urban planning, craft and long-distance trade at Mohenjo-daro and related sites — a Bronze Age civilisation on this river, not a modern national origin myth.

Gandhara

Buddhist monastic and urban culture in the northwest; sculpture and learning centred on Taxila and neighbouring complexes.

Sultanate & Mughal

Persianate courts, Sufi networks, mosque-fort-garden architecture, and the mixing of languages that later feed Urdu and regional literatures.

Sikh-era Punjab

A political and architectural layer in Punjab’s historic cities, still visible in gurdwaras and forts.

Colonial period

Print, census, railways, new towns, English education, and reform movements that reshaped Muslim, Hindu and Sikh public life.

1947

Partition, migration, and the making of a new state cultural policy — Radio Pakistan, later PTV, academies, and the trauma of displaced communities. Continuity and rupture together.

1970s–1990s

PTV drama as national literature-in-dialogue; pop and film; shifting official language and cultural policies; video cassette circulation.

2000s–digital era

Private television, then social media and streaming; NAPA and expanded arts education; UNESCO ICH listings; urban café and fashion economies; craft and language pressure in the same years.

Stewards

Museums, archives & cultural institutions

Lok Virsa

The National Institute of Folk & Traditional Heritage (Lok Virsa), established in 1974 and made autonomous in 2002, sits at Shakarparian in Islamabad. Its official mandate is research, collection, documentation, preservation and promotion of tangible and intangible folk heritage. The Heritage Museum (Pakistan National Museum of Ethnology) is described by Lok Virsa as the country’s first state ethnology museum, renovated in the 2000s, presenting living traditions rather than only archaeology. A Heritage Library serves researchers. Lok Virsa has published recordings and books, held artisan festivals (including Lok Mela), and documented regional crafts. This summary is original; it does not copy the institute’s website prose. Official site: lokvirsa.org.pk.

Other verified bodies

  • Pakistan National Council of the Arts (PNCA), F-5/1 Islamabad — National Art Gallery, performing-arts programming, puppet theatre.
  • National Academy of Performing Arts (NAPA), Karachi — theatre and music training; Zia Mohyeddin founding president.
  • Pakistan Academy of Letters and the National Language Promotion Department — literary and Urdu-language work (see Literature archive).
  • National Heritage & Culture Division — federal administrative home for several cultural bodies.

Provincial museums, arts councils and university departments do local work this national page cannot inventory without error. The Pakistan Monument Museum is a separate Islamabad display space adjoining the monument.

Protecting living heritage

Pressures include urbanisation, language shift toward Urdu and English in some urban children, workshop decline, and the loss of instruments (Boreendo’s UNESCO urgent listing is evidence, not a slogan). Safeguarding is documentation, teaching, paying artisans, and community consent — not only museums. UNESCO listing helps some files and can freeze others into “heritage performance.” Evidence, not panic, is the right tone.

Crafts & creative economy

Handicrafts, textiles, music, publishing, film, design and cultural tourism are livelihoods. This is not an economic survey. It is a reminder that culture is work as well as identity.

Cultural glossary

Ajrak
Block-printed, resist-dyed cloth particularly associated with Sindh; a cultural marker as well as a textile.
Hujra
A guest and gathering space in many Pashtun communities; social architecture, not a hotel.
Mela
A fair, often mixing devotion, commerce and performance.
Mushaira
A gathering for the recitation of poetry, especially Urdu.
Urs
The death-anniversary festival of a Sufi saint, held at the shrine.
Qawwali
A South Asian Muslim devotional musical assembly, historically shrine-based.
Dhamal
Drum-led circling and movement at some Sufi shrines, especially in Sindh.
Bhangra
A Punjabi harvest and celebratory dance, now also a staged and diasporic genre.
Giddha
A Punjabi women’s circle dance in many community settings.
Attan
A circle dance associated with various Pashtun gatherings; styles differ.
Phulkari
Flower-embroidery textile tradition associated with parts of Punjab.
Ralli
Patchwork quilting associated especially with Sindh.
Dastan
A long oral narrative or romance cycle.
Baithak
A sitting-room or gathering for conversation, music or teaching.
Mehndi
Henna, and the pre-wedding gathering at which it is applied, in many communities.
Jirga
A council or assembly for dispute discussion, historically important in some Pashtun and related settings; not a court of the modern state.

زبانیں: مردم شماری 2023 کے مطابق مادری زبان کے طور پر پنجابی، پشتو، سندھی، سرائیکی اور اردو بڑی حصے ہیں، مگر بروشسکی، وخی، کھوار، ہندکو، براہوئی اور کیلاشہ جیسی زبانیں وادیوں کی پہچان ہیں۔ موسیقی قوالی، غزل، لوک گیت اور جدید ریکارڈنگ سب ایک ہی نقشے پر آتی ہے۔ کھانے میدان، ساحل، ریگستان اور پہاڑ کے حساب سے بدلتے ہیں۔ لباس میں شلوار قمیض عام ہے مگر اجرک، پھلکاری، بلوچی کڑھائی اور کالاش و چترالی پوشاک علاقائی ہیں۔

تہوار عید، عرس، میلے، کالاش کے موسمی تہوار اور کچھ علاقوں میں نوروز شامل ہیں؛ بسنت لاہور میں قانون کے تحت محدود رہا ہے۔ لوک ورثہ اسلام آباد میں لوک ورثے کی دستاویز بناتا ہے۔ یونیسکو کے عالمی ورثہ پر پاکستان کے چھ درج مقامات ہیں؛ عارضی فہرست درج شدہ فہرست نہیں۔ یہ صفحہ کسی صوبے کو دوسرے سے “زیادہ ثقافتی” نہیں کہتا۔
